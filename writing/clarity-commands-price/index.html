Brand positioning: why clarity commands price, and what 91 industrial homepages reveal

By Anne Becheru, founder of Brave New Media.

Published 3 Nov 2026 · Updated 9 Nov 202618 min read

Start chapter 1 →Key findings ↓

Brand positioning is the place a business holds in a buyer's mind compared with the alternatives: who it is for, what it does, and why it is the better choice. The term was made popular by Al Ries and Jack Trout, whose 1981 book Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind placed the position where its title says: in the buyer's mind, not in the product.[1] The Hive covers the history in its entry on the positioning era.

This essay brings together three bodies of evidence on why a clear position supports a higher price: research on processing fluency, behavioural data on how little of a web page gets read, and our own study of the homepages of 91 US mid-cap manufacturing and supply-chain companies. We close with a framework, the Clarity Ladder, a positioning statement template and a 5-second test.

Idea in brief

The problem. Buyers decide fast and read little. In one large browsing study, visitors had time to read at most 28% of the words on an average page view, and 20% was more likely.[4] If your position is not clear in that slice, the buyer falls back on the one thing they can compare: price.

Why it happens. People judge what is easy to process as safer and better. A $1,000 basket of new shares with the easiest names out-earned the hardest-named basket by $112 on day one of trading.[2]

The gap. In our scan of 64 industrial homepages that returned readable text, only 31% said both what the company makes and who it serves within the first 50 words. The median homepage scored 34.8 on the Flesch Reading Ease scale, and 81% used the word "solution" or "solutions".

The answer. Climb the Clarity Ladder in order: say what you make, say who it is for, say it first, say it plainly, then say why you.

Key findings

The essay in 2 minutes
  1. Finding 1

    Fluency moves money, mostly in the short run.

    In Alter and Oppenheimer's PNAS study of 89 NYSE listings from 1990 to 2004, name complexity predicted weaker performance after 1 day and 1 week of trading, but not reliably at 6 months or 1 year.[2]

  2. Finding 298.5%

    Pages are screened in seconds.

    Across 205,873 web pages with at least 10,000 visits each, 98.5% showed "negative aging": visitors were most likely to leave early, and the leaving rate fell over time.[3]

  3. Finding 311.5

    Industrial homepages are hard to read.

    Of 59 homepages with enough text to score, the median Flesch-Kincaid grade was 11.5, and 47% scored at grade 12 or above. None scored 70 or higher on the Flesch Reading Ease scale.

  4. Finding 439%

    Most do not say who they serve, early.

    In the first 50 words of 64 homepages, 63% named what the company makes, 39% named who it serves, and 30% did neither.

  5. Finding 594%

    Generic words crowd out specific ones.

    94% of the 64 homepages used at least one of our list of generic claims. The median scored homepage used 19.1 of them per 1,000 words.

Chapter 1 · 1 min

What brand positioning is

A position is not a slogan. It is the short answer a buyer would give if a colleague asked, "What do they do, and why them?" Ries and Trout's point was that the position lives in the prospect's head, so the job is to make that answer easy to form and easy to repeat.[1] The Hive's guide to positioning in marketing goes deeper on how to choose one.

A clear position answers three questions in plain words:

1. Who is it for? A specific kind of buyer, not "businesses" or "customers worldwide".

2. What does it do? The product or service, named the way a buyer would name it.

3. Why you? The reason you are the better choice for that buyer, including against doing nothing.

Chapter 2 · 2 min

The evidence, part 1: fluency changes what things are worth

Processing fluency research asks whether the ease of taking in information changes what people believe about the thing itself. The clearest test with real money comes from the stock market. Adam Alter and Daniel Oppenheimer studied shares just after they began trading, when investors know least about a company.[2] In their second study they took 89 shares that began trading on the NYSE between 1990 and 2004 and measured how complex each company name was. The more complex the name, the worse the share did after 1 day and after 1 week of trading. At 6 months and 1 year the effect was no longer reliable, although it pointed the same way.

To show the size of the effect, the authors compared $1,000 invested in the 10 most fluently named shares with $1,000 in the 10 least fluent.

Their third study used ticker codes. Two coders sorted 665 NYSE shares by whether the ticker could be pronounced as a word, and the authors repeated the test on 116 AMEX shares.

Read the two figures together. Fluency mattered most when buyers knew least, on the first day, and its pull faded as real information arrived. That is exactly the position a new supplier is in with a buyer who has never heard of it. In the first meeting, or the first visit to a homepage, ease of understanding carries more weight than it will once the buyer has seen your work.

A second line of evidence comes from Hyunjin Song and Norbert Schwarz at the University of Michigan.[5] Students given exercise instructions in plain Arial expected the routine to take less time and feel easier than students given the same text in a hard-to-read brush font, and were more willing to do it. A sushi recipe showed the same pattern. People judge how hard a task is by how hard its directions are to read. For a supplier, the description of working with you is the direction.

Limits of the fluency evidence

  • The stock study covers one market and one period, and the authors call the effect size small.
  • The font experiments used students and lab tasks. We could not access the full paper, so we report only the published direction of the results.
  • None of these studies measured B2B prices. They show that ease of processing shifts judgment under uncertainty, the condition a first-time buyer is in.

Chapter 3 · 1 min

The evidence, part 2: how little of a page gets read

Chao Liu, Ryen White and Susan Dumais of Microsoft Research analysed browsing logs for 205,873 web pages, each with at least 10,000 recorded visits, and published the results at the SIGIR conference in 2010.[3] For 80% of pages, the typical visit length was no more than 70 seconds. And 98.5% of pages showed what the authors call "negative aging": the chance of leaving is highest at the start of a visit and falls the longer someone stays. In plain terms, visitors screen a page first and read it second.

Jakob Nielsen of Nielsen Norman Group, writing about that study in 2011, concluded that the first 10 seconds of a visit are critical to whether people stay, and that a page must communicate its value proposition within 10 seconds to earn minutes of attention.[8]

An earlier study measured how much gets read once people stay. Harald Weinreich and colleagues recorded the normal browsing of 25 people, and Nielsen analysed 45,237 of their page views in 2008.[4]

The data is old and the sample small, but the direction is clear: most of a page goes unread. In our sample, the median industrial homepage that returned text carried 371.5 visible words in its raw HTML. If a buyer reads a fifth of that, they read about 74 words. That is why our study looks so closely at the first 50.

Chapter 4 · 4 min

Original research: how clearly do industrial homepages state their position?

The studies above describe buyers in general. We wanted to know how the companies in our own sector present themselves. On 3 November 2026 we fetched the homepages of 91 US mid-cap manufacturing and supply-chain companies, the same sample we used in our study of what AI says about you: every company in the S&P MidCap 400 and S&P SmallCap 600 lists classed under eleven manufacturing and freight sub-industries. The full method is at the end of this essay.

Who we could measure

67 of the 91 homepages (74%) returned a readable page to our script, which named itself as a research bot. Of those, 3 contained no visible text in their raw HTML because their content loads with JavaScript, leaving 64 homepages with text. For readability scores we used the 59 homepages with at least 100 words of prose. Every percentage below states its base.

Finding 1: industrial homepages read at a university level

We scored each homepage with the Flesch Reading Ease formula and the Flesch-Kincaid grade formula.[6][7] Both use the same two inputs: words per sentence and syllables per word.

The median score was 34.8, and half the homepages fell between 25.9 and 44.2. The lowest scored 11.8 and the highest 67.1. On the grade scale, the median was 11.5, half fell between grade 10.0 and 13.2, and 28 of 59 homepages (47%) scored at grade 12 or above. Only 6 (10%) scored below grade 9.

The cause is not long sentences. The median homepage averaged 13.6 words per sentence, which is short. It was long words: 1.86 syllables per word, with about a quarter (25.6%) of all words running to three syllables or more. Words like "comprehensive", "infrastructure" and "innovative" do the damage.

Tip: Paste your homepage text into a free readability checker. If the grade is above 10, swap the three-syllable words for the ones a buyer would use on the phone.

Finding 2: fewer than a third say what they make and who it is for, up front

Readability measures how hard the words are. It does not measure whether they say anything. So we read the first 50 visible words of each of the 64 homepages with text, starting at the main headline, and coded two things by hand: does it name what the company makes or does, as a product or service category, and does it name who it serves, as a buyer type or industry? "Solutions" did not count as a product. "Customers" and "the world" did not count as a buyer.

Product categories were named more often (40 of 64, 63%) than buyers (25 of 64, 39%). Leaving out the buyer is the more costly gap, because the buyer is the one reading. A visitor who cannot see themselves in the first lines has to guess whether you work with companies like theirs.

In 13 of the 64 homepages (20%), the first 50 words were not about the offer at all: company news (4), reports (3), anniversaries (2), site notices (2), careers (1) and investor results (1). Each is useful to someone. None is what a new buyer needs first.

The homepages that did both were plain. JBT Marel offers technology "for food and beverage producers". Griffon calls itself "the largest manufacturer and marketer of sectional garage doors and rolling steel doors in North America". Donaldson offers "filtration for heavy-duty equipment". None is clever. Each tells a buyer at once whether to keep going.

Finding 3: the most common word is "solutions"

We also counted generic claims: words any company could use about itself. Our list held 38 such words and forms, fixed before scoring, including "solutions", "innovative", "leading", "world-class", "seamless" and "trusted".

60 of 64 homepages (94%) used at least one word on the list. Across the 59 scored homepages, the median rate was 19.1 generic words per 1,000 words of text, about one in every 52 words.

These words are not wrong, just shared. When 52 of 64 companies in one sector sell "solutions", the word tells a buyer nothing about any of them. It also hurts readability: "solutions" has three syllables, "innovation" four. The specific word, "pumps", "railcars", "garage doors", is shorter, clearer and yours.

Limits of our study

  • One day and one sample of US-listed companies, many of them diversified groups with a harder positioning job than a single-product supplier.
  • Raw HTML only, so a browser may show different text. Readability formulas measure length, not meaning.
  • One coder applied the first-50-words rules. The coding sheet is published so others can check it.
  • We did not measure prices or win rates, so we cannot say these homepages cost their owners money.

Chapter 6 · 2 min

How to write a positioning statement

A positioning statement is a one- or two-sentence internal summary of your position, the reference point every page, pitch and proposal should match. A widely used template comes from Geoffrey Moore's book Crossing the Chasm. A plain version:

For [a specific buyer] who [has a specific problem], [your business] is the [type of offer] that [the main result they get]. Unlike [the main alternative], we [the one reason to choose you].

A worked example

This case is made up, but it's typical. Say a 40-person parts manufacturer has a homepage that offers "CNC machining, fabrication, assembly and finishing for all industries." That is four services and no buyer, so it would land in the "What they make only" row of Figure 5.

After an afternoon's work, its positioning statement reads:

For food-packaging equipment builders who lose weeks when supplier parts fail inspection, the shop machines stainless components that pass on first delivery. Unlike general job shops, it only makes parts for food-processing machinery, so it already knows the hygiene standards, the materials and the tolerances.

The shop now has one buyer, one problem and one reason. An equipment builder is no longer comparing it with the cheapest quote. They are comparing it with the cost of a late machine. A sharp unique selling proposition says what you are best at. A clear position says you are best at it for someone like them.

Write it in five steps

1. List your best five customers. Look for what they share: industry, size, problem.

2. Name the shared problem in their words from sales calls and RFQs.

3. Name the real alternative: a bigger competitor, a cheaper overseas supplier, doing it in-house or doing nothing.

4. Find your one reason the alternative cannot easily copy: a method, a certification, a track record.

5. Fill in the template and cut every word a competitor could also claim, starting with the words in Figure 6.

Tip: If your "why you" line could be pasted onto a competitor's site without anyone noticing, it is not a reason yet.

Turn the statement into the first screen

The headline and the two lines under it should carry the buyer, the offer and the result, at grade 10 or below. Then keep it the same everywhere a buyer meets you. The Hive's entry on distinctive brand assets explains why repeated cues help buyers remember you.

Chapter 7 · 1 min

The 5-second test

The 5-second test checks whether your brand positioning survives the screening the research describes.[3][4] Show five people who don't know your business the top of your homepage for five seconds, on a phone if most visitors use one. Hide it and ask: Who is this for? What do they make? Why choose them? Write down their exact words without hinting. If four of five get all three right, you are clear.

If they say "some kind of solutions company", fix rung 1. If they name the wrong buyer, fix rung 2. If they remember your news, fix rung 3. If they can't say why you, fix rung 5.

Chapter 8 · 1 min

What this means for leaders

For a manufacturer or a logistics company, positioning is a pricing tool before it is a marketing one. Engineering checks the spec, procurement checks the price, finance checks the risk. A position one person can explain in a sentence is one the whole committee can defend. A vague one leaves procurement with only the number at the bottom of the quote.

The fluency research adds a timing point: ease mattered most on day one, when investors knew least.[2] A supplier entering a new market or buyer segment is always on day one with someone.

Clarity is also what lets a company go broad. We build AI flows that take one fixed positioning statement and adapt it into market-specific pages, sales emails and proposals, checked against the same ladder, so a company can reach more buyers without hiring at the same rate. A vague position copied into ten markets is ten vague positions. A clear one becomes a customer base. Our case studies show how that looks in practice.

Chapter 9 · 1 min

Common mistakes

  • Leading with the company, not the buyer. Mission statements and anniversaries are about you. The first lines should be about who you serve.
  • Using the sector's shared vocabulary. "Solutions" appeared on 81% of the homepages we read. A word everyone uses cannot set you apart.
  • Writing for the expert who already knows you. Specialist terms are fine for a spec sheet. The first screen is read by strangers, often in procurement.
  • Putting the position behind JavaScript. Three homepages in our sample showed no text at all without it. Some crawlers may see the same blank.

Chapter 10 · 1 min

Methodology

Sample and fetch. All 91 companies on Wikipedia's S&P MidCap 400 and S&P SmallCap 600 constituent pages in eleven GICS Industrials manufacturing and freight sub-industries, with websites from Wikidata (P856), five corrected by hand. On 3 November 2026 a script requested each homepage once as "BraveNewResearch/1.0" with a link to brave-new.com, read raw HTML without JavaScript, and did not retry blocked requests. 67 returned HTTP 200. After removing scripts, navigation, headers, footers, buttons, cookie banners, hidden elements and labels such as "Learn more", 64 had visible text.

Readability. Flesch Reading Ease = 206.835 minus 1.015 times words per sentence minus 84.6 times syllables per word.[6] Flesch-Kincaid grade = 0.39 times words per sentence plus 11.8 times syllables per word minus 15.59.[7] Sentences end at full stops, question marks and exclamation marks, and a block without one, such as a heading, counts as one sentence. We scored blocks of five or more words and kept homepages with at least 100 such words (59). Syllables come from the Carnegie Mellon University Pronouncing Dictionary, with a vowel-group rule for other words.

First 50 words and generic claims. The first 50 visible words from the first H1 headline were coded by hand for a product or service category and a buyer type or industry. Generic claims were matched against a fixed list of 38 words and forms. Scripts, the coding sheet and the data behind every figure are published with this essay.

Chapter 11 · 1 min

Frequently asked questions

What is brand positioning in simple terms?

Brand positioning is the place your business holds in a buyer's mind compared with the other options. In simple terms, it is the short answer a customer would give if a colleague asked what you do and why they chose you. A clear position names who you serve, what you make or do, and why you are the better choice for that buyer.

Does clear messaging really let you charge more?

Clear messaging lowers a buyer's sense of risk, and lower risk makes a higher price easier to accept. Research on processing fluency shows that easy-to-process information is judged more favourably, especially when people know little, as with newly listed shares on their first day of trading. When buyers understand exactly what you do for companies like theirs, they compare you with the cost of their problem.

How readable should a B2B homepage be?

There is no official standard, but lower grades are easier for everyone, including experts in a hurry. In our scan of 59 industrial homepages, the median Flesch-Kincaid grade was 11.5 and only 10% scored below grade 9. Aim for grade 9 or below in the first screen. Keep technical detail for product pages, where specialists go looking for it.

How can an industrial company stand out from competitors?

Name your product the way buyers search for it, name the buyer or industry you serve in the first two lines, and drop the words every competitor uses. In our scan, 81% of industrial homepages used "solutions". Then add one specific reason to choose you, backed by proof such as a certification, a delivery record or a named reference.

Your next step

This week, copy the first 50 words of your homepage into a document. Circle the product name and the buyer. If either is missing, or if a generic word sits where a specific one should be, rewrite those 50 words using your positioning statement, then run the 5-second test with five people who don't know you. Their answers tell you which rung of the ladder to fix.
