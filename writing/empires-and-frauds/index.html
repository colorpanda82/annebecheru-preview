Fake it till you make it: where a big story becomes fraud, in data

By Anne Becheru, founder of Brave New Media.

Published 3 Nov 2026 · Updated 9 Nov 202620 min read

Start chapter 1 →Key findings ↓

"Fake it till you make it" is the advice to act more capable or confident than you feel until the real thing catches up. Dictionaries file it under psychology: pretending to be a certain way until you become that way.[1] Applied to confidence, it is how many founders grow into the job. Applied to facts that investors and buyers rely on, it is how stories turn into securities fraud. The line falls where a story presents as true today something the business cannot verify, to people who are handing over money because they believe it.

This essay brings together four bodies of evidence: the court and regulator record of Theranos, Nikola, FTX and WeWork; research on how much fraud goes undetected; twelve years of the US Securities and Exchange Commission's enforcement statistics; and our own study of ten years of Wikipedia attention to these companies and their founders. We close with a framework, the Claim Ladder, and a method any founder, marketer or buyer can use this week.

Idea in brief

The problem. Every growing company tells a story that runs ahead of its numbers. Most of that stretch is ambition. Some of it moves money on claims nobody can check, and that is where the legal and reputational risk sits.

Why it happens. Fraud is mostly invisible while it runs. Researchers estimate that in normal times only about one-third of corporate frauds are detected, and that about 10% of large publicly traded companies are committing securities fraud in an average year.[9] A story that is not checked can keep growing.

The gap (our data). In our study of eight Wikipedia articles, attention to the companies faded after exposure, but attention to the founders stayed or grew. Elizabeth Holmes's article drew 41 times its pre-exposure monthly average in March 2022, more than six years after the first investigation, and almost 11 million views that year.

The answer. Separate what is true today from what you are building toward, and say which is which. Then climb the Claim Ladder: move each claim from "we say so" toward "a stranger can test it."

Chapter 1 · 1 min

What "fake it till you make it" means, and where it breaks

The phrase is useful in one narrow sense: acting more confident than you feel, so you can learn on the job. Every new founder pitches before feeling ready. Faking facts is a different act: telling an investor that a product works, that revenue exists or that a partner signed when none of it is true. Those claims move money, and once money moves on a false statement of fact, the problem is legal, not mental.

The SEC said it plainly when it charged Theranos in 2018. Jina Choi, then director of its San Francisco office: "Innovators who seek to revolutionize and disrupt an industry must tell investors the truth about what their technology can do today, not just what they hope it might do someday."[2]

Chapter 2 · 2 min

The evidence, part 1: claim versus record

Regulators and courts set out, in writing, what each company said and what was true. We read the primary documents for four cases and put the claims beside the record.

Theranos: the story outran the machine

On 14 March 2018, the SEC charged Theranos, Elizabeth Holmes and former president Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani with raising more than $700 million through "an elaborate, years-long fraud." Holmes settled without admitting or denying the allegations and accepted a $500,000 penalty and a 10-year officer and director bar.[2]

The criminal case was narrower than many remember. On 3 January 2022, a jury convicted Holmes on four counts of defrauding investors, involving wire transfers totalling more than $140 million, and acquitted her on the patient-related counts. She was sentenced to 135 months in federal prison. Balwani, tried separately, received 155 months.[3]

The story was not the crime. Selling it as current fact, to people investing on it, was.

Nikola: the claims went out on social media

In July 2021, the SEC charged Nikola's founder Trevor Milton with fraud for "repeatedly disseminating false and misleading information," typically through social media, about the company's products and technology. The SEC's regional director put the lesson in one line: "Public company officials cannot say whatever they want on social media without regard for the federal securities laws."[5]

The complaint dates the turn precisely. On 10 September 2020, a research firm holding a short position published a report saying Milton had misrepresented the business, and Nikola's stock fell about 24%. Milton resigned ten days later.[6]

FTX: the story said customer money was safe

On 12 June 2026, the Second Circuit upheld Sam Bankman-Fried's conviction on seven counts of fraud and conspiracy, his 25-year sentence and a forfeiture of approximately $11 billion.[4] It called the evidence, "conservatively stated, robust," and wrote the sentence that best defines where a story becomes fraud:

"While he was publicly reassuring customers, investors, and regulators that FTX customer funds were safe, he was simultaneously using FTX as his own personal piggy bank, spending customer funds on real estate, political contributions, and investments."[4]

The collapse took nine days. On 2 November 2022, a version of Alameda's balance sheet was published by a cryptocurrency news site, triggering customer withdrawals. On 11 November, FTX filed for bankruptcy because it could not meet them.[4]

Chapter 3 · 1 min

The evidence, part 2: a big story with no fraud finding

Not every collapse is a crime. No court found WeWork's IPO to be a fraud. Its prospectus, filed on 14 August 2019, told the story first and the numbers second.[7]

Revenue roughly doubled every year, and so did the net loss. All of it was disclosed, public investors read it, and on 30 September 2019 the company filed to withdraw the registration.[8] A mission statement cannot carry a balance sheet. When the story and the numbers disagree in a document people can read, the numbers win, and the reputation risk is the size of the gap between them.

Chapter 4 · 1 min

The evidence, part 3: how much fraud goes unseen

The cases above were caught. Most are not. Dyck, Morse and Zingales estimate that only one-third of corporate frauds are detected in normal times, and that about 10% of large publicly traded firms commit securities fraud in an average year, with a 95% range of 7% to 14%.[9] They used Arthur Andersen's demise, which put its former clients under added scrutiny and exposed frauds already under way. They also estimate that fraud destroys 1.6% of equity value each year, equal to $830 billion in 2021.[9] For a buyer or an investor, the absence of a scandal is weak evidence that a story is true.

In the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners' 2024 study of fraud against people's own organisations, owners and executives caused median losses more than seven times those of employees.[10] The 2026 edition, covering 2,402 cases in 143 countries, found a median loss of $104,000 per case, a typical fraud lasting 12 months before detection, and 43% of frauds detected through a tip, more than half of them from employees.[11]

The people who tell a company's story to the outside world are the people whose fraud costs most, and the most common way fraud is found is that someone close to the facts notices the mismatch and says so.

Limits of this evidence

  • The Dyck, Morse and Zingales estimates rest on one natural experiment in large publicly traded companies and are a lower bound.
  • The ACFE studies describe detected fraud only, and the 2024 and 2026 samples differ, so they are not a trend.
  • Consumer fraud is a separate problem, but it shows the same pull of a good story. US consumers reported losing $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025, and investment scams accounted for more than $7.9 billion of it, about half the total.[12]

Chapter 5 · 4 min

Original research: what the record and the attention data show

We measured two things a founder can learn from directly: how the regulator's activity has moved, and what happens to public attention after a story is exposed. Both use free, public data. The full method is at the end of this essay.

Study A: twelve years of SEC enforcement results

Each year the SEC publishes its enforcement results for the fiscal year ending 30 September. We read every release from fiscal 2014 to 2025 and took the totals exactly as stated.

The count peaked at 868 in fiscal 2016 and stayed between 697 and 862 through fiscal 2023. Then it fell to 583 and 456, the lowest in the series and 47% below the peak. Standalone cases fell from 548 to 303, a drop of 45%.

In its fiscal 2025 release, the SEC said that about two-thirds of its standalone actions involved charges against one or more individuals, and that nearly nine in ten standalone actions filed under its current leadership did.[13] A falling case count is not permission. The cases that remain are more likely to be about the person telling the story.

The money needs care. For eight years, orders sat between about $3.8 billion and $4.7 billion. Then single cases began to dominate. The SEC said about 56% of the record $8.2 billion in fiscal 2024 came from one judgment, against Terraform Labs and its founder.[14] The fiscal 2025 headline of $17.9 billion includes amounts the SEC deemed satisfied by other courts' orders and its long-running Stanford litigation. On the basis the SEC itself offered as comparable, fiscal 2025 orders were $2.7 billion: $1.4 billion in disgorgement and interest and $1.3 billion in penalties.[13]

A few founder-led frauds can account for most of a year's money, and the headline number in any report depends on definitions, the same lesson this essay draws for founders' own numbers.

Study B: what happens to attention after exposure

We pulled monthly English Wikipedia pageviews from July 2015 to December 2025 for four companies and their founders, and compared the month each story broke with the months before it.

The first spike tracked how sudden the end was: FTX failed nine days after its trouble became public, and attention jumped about thirty-fold. The later result matters more. Three years on, attention to FTX and Nikola had fallen to 0.7 and 0.3 times its earlier level, while their founders' articles held at 1.3 and 1.0 times. WeWork and Adam Neumann, with no fraud finding, held at 3.5 and 9.2 times.

Theranos is the clearest case of a story that outlived the company. The founder's article out-drew the company's every year. Attention peaked not when the fraud was first reported, but in 2022, the year of the verdict and the sentencing and of a dramatised television series that began on 3 March 2022.[3][17] In March 2022 alone, the Holmes article drew about 2.84 million views, 41 times its average in the months before the first investigation. Over the full period, the founder's article drew 36.5 million views against 14.4 million for the company.

This is the reputational arithmetic of faking facts. A company that fails is forgotten. A founder whose story is shown to be false becomes the story, for years, on the platforms buyers, journalists and AI tools read first. That is the difference between brand and reputation: you write the brand story, and other people write the verdict on it.

Chapter 6 · 1 min

A framework: the Claim Ladder

The evidence points to one discipline: know how far each claim can be checked, and say it at that strength. Each rung up the Claim Ladder makes a claim harder to fake and easier to trust.

RungWhat it meansExampleEvidence
5. TestableA buyer or investor can test it themselvesA trial run on the buyer's own parts or dataTheranos ran most patient tests on other makers' machines while claiming its own; a test by the buyer would have shown it.[2]
4. Public recordIt sits in a filing, audit or court recordAudited accounts, a filed prospectusWeWork's losses were in its S-1, and investors read them.[7]
3. Third-party confirmedSomeone you do not control confirms itA customer reference the buyer picks43% of occupational frauds are found through tips, most from people close to the facts.[11]
2. DocumentedYou hold a document that backs it upA signed contract, a delivery recordThe Nikola complaint compares public claims with delivery dates.[6]

A healthy story uses every rung, but it labels them. Goals live at rung 1, and that is fine if they are called goals. The danger is a rung 1 claim presented as if it sat at rung 4.

Chapter 7 · 1 min

How to tell a big story without faking it

Step 1: Split today from tomorrow

Write two lists. "True today" holds only facts you could prove to a stranger this week. "Where we are going" holds the vision. Both belong in your pitch, but nothing moves from the second list to the first until it has earned it.

A stretched claim gets stretched again in each new market, translation and local sales deck. When we build AI flows that take a company's story to more markets and more buyers, the "true today" list is the source the flow draws from. The flow can then scale the story without scaling the risk.

Step 2: Label projections as projections

Every forecast should say it is a forecast and show its main assumptions. A number stated without its "if" is a promise you may not keep. Theranos's 2014 revenue projection is the extreme case: a number stated as an expectation, about a thousand times larger than what arrived.[2]

Step 3: Give proof others can test

The strongest proof is something you do not control: a reference call, audited figures, an independent test. A demo you stage yourself is the weakest, because it can always be arranged to work.

Tip: Before a big pitch, ask someone outside your company to verify your top five claims using only public information and the documents you would share. Wherever they get stuck, investors will too.

Step 4: Say what you do not know, and give every claim an owner

Naming your risks builds more trust than hiding them. The Hive's entry on trust architecture shows how to arrange evidence so the hardest-to-fake proof carries the most weight. Then make one person responsible for backing each public claim with a document, and review the list every quarter.

Chapter 8 · 1 min

How buyers and investors can spot a "fake it till you make it" story

If you are being pitched, separate confidence from facts. Most fraud is not detected, so do not assume someone else has checked.[9]

Questions to ask

  • "Can I test it myself?" Ask to run the product on your own case, not watch a demo.
  • "What is true today, and what is a projection?" A good founder answers this cleanly.
  • "Can I speak to a customer you did not pick?" References chosen by the founder tell you less.

Watch for secrecy presented as a competitive advantage, metrics nobody else uses, pressure to decide quickly, and big claims made mainly on social media rather than in documents. The SEC's Nikola case was built on that channel.[5]

This case is made up, but it's typical. Say a founder is pitching software that "cuts logistics costs by 30%." A careful investor asks where the 30% comes from. The honest founder says: "One pilot customer, over four months. You can call them. We do not yet know if it holds at scale." The founder who is faking it says: "Our clients consistently see 30% or more," and the client list turns out to be that one pilot. Same number. Only one survives a phone call.

Trust is scarce, as the Edelman Trust Barometer has shown for years, so a business that makes verification easy stands out.

Chapter 9 · 1 min

What this means for leaders

We work across supply chain and manufacturing, where claims get tested fast: a buyer can visit the plant, call a reference and pull a delivery record. The stories to worry about are the ones where every question gets a better story instead of a document.

For a company that wants to grow its customer base or enter new markets, the evidence points one way. Expansion multiplies your story: more sales decks, more languages, more distributors, more AI tools summarising you for buyers who have never met you. A claim that sits at rung 1 in your home market becomes a liability in ten markets. A claim that sits at rung 4 or 5 becomes an asset, because every new buyer can check it.

Our attention data adds a second point: when a story is shown to be false, the cost lands on the people who told it and stays on the open web for years.

Chapter 10 · 1 min

Common mistakes honest founders make

Most founders never come close to fraud, but smaller mistakes erode trust:

  • Present tense for future plans. "We serve the whole of Europe" when you have signed one client in Spain.
  • Borrowed logos. Showing a company's logo because someone there once took a call.
  • Vanity metrics. Sign-ups instead of paying customers, without saying which is which.
  • Rounding up. Calling $80,000 "six figures" or 40 customers "dozens of enterprises."
  • Never updating the story. A claim that was true last year and quietly is not anymore.

Chapter 11 · 1 min

Methodology

Case documents. Figure 1 and the case sections use only the primary documents in references 2 to 8. Every quotation is word for word.

Study A: SEC enforcement results. On 3 November 2026 we downloaded the SEC's annual enforcement results press release for each fiscal year from 2014 to 2025 (references 13 to 15) from sec.gov with the user agent "BraveNewResearch/1.0 (+https://brave-new.com; research)". All twelve returned the page. Fiscal 2014's standalone count and fiscal 2021's money ordered come from the following year's release. Where the SEC wrote "approximately" or "more than," we report the stated figure and flag it.

Study B: Wikipedia attention. On 3 November 2026 we pulled monthly pageviews by human users from the Wikimedia pageviews API for eight English Wikipedia articles, July 2015 to December 2025. For FTX we added the earlier title "FTX (company)," because the article's main title changed in late 2022. Event months were fixed in advance from dated sources: October 2015 (the Wall Street Journal investigation of Theranos[18]), September 2020 (the short-seller report on Nikola[6]), November 2022 (FTX's bankruptcy[4]) and September 2019 (WeWork's withdrawal of its IPO filing[8]). The baseline is the average of the up to twelve months before the event month. "Three years later" is the average of months 25 to 36 after the event. Television premiere dates come from Wikidata.

Limits. Pageviews measure attention, not opinion, on English Wikipedia only. Some baselines cover three months, because the API starts in July 2015 and the Nikola article in June 2020. SEC definitions changed over time, and we report them as stated. Our case set is small and chosen because it is well documented, so it is not a sample of all frauds. The scripts, raw data and a CSV for every figure are published with this essay.

Chapter 12 · 1 min

Frequently asked questions

Is "fake it till you make it" good advice for entrepreneurs?

It is good advice for confidence and bad advice for facts. Acting more confident than you feel while you learn a role is normal. Presenting goals, revenue or product capabilities as real when they are not is different, and once others rely on those claims to invest or buy, it can become fraud.

Where is the line between storytelling and fraud?

The line falls where your story presents something as true today that you cannot verify, to people making financial decisions because of it. A vision is fine when it is labelled as a goal. The SEC's warning after Theranos was to tell investors what the technology can do today, not just what you hope it might do someday.

How common is corporate fraud?

More common than the headlines suggest. A 2024 study in the Review of Accounting Studies estimates that about 10% of large publicly traded companies commit securities fraud in an average year, and that only about one-third of corporate frauds are detected in normal times. It puts the cost at 1.6% of equity value a year, equal to $830 billion in 2021.

How can investors spot a startup that is faking it?

Ask for proof you can test yourself, not demos the founder controls. Speak to customers the founder did not choose. Ask what is true today and what is a projection, and what would make the business fail. Watch for secrecy presented as strategy, invented metrics, pressure to decide fast and claims made mainly on social media.

Can a marketing agency help a company tell a big story honestly?

Yes. An agency can sort every claim by how well it can be checked, rewrite goals so they read as goals, and build proof buyers can test.
