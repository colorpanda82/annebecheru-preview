Brand storytelling: why empires start as stories, and what 59 industrial about pages leave out

By Anne Becheru, founder of Brave New Media.

Published 3 Nov 2026 · Updated 9 Nov 202618 min read

Start chapter 1 →Key findings ↓

Brand storytelling is the practice of explaining a business as a story, with a person, a problem and a turning point, rather than as a list of facts, so that customers can remember it and retell it. The economic case for stories was set out by Robert Shiller in his 2017 presidential address to the American Economic Association, "Narrative Economics", which argued that popular stories spread through an economy the way a disease spreads through a population, and move spending and investing as they go.[1]

This essay brings together field and lab data on how a story changes what people pay and give, Shiller's model of how stories spread and fade, and our own study of the about and history pages of 59 US mid-cap manufacturing and supply-chain companies. We close with a framework, the Story Ladder, and a five-part method for writing a brand story.

Idea in brief

The problem. Most industrial companies are old enough to have a real story. The median company in our sample was founded in 1944, and 30 of 77 with a recorded founding date are at least 100 years old. Yet their about pages mostly read as timelines about themselves.

Why it matters. Stories change value. Thrift-store objects bought for an average of $1.29 sold for an average of $36.13 once writers gave each one an invented story.[2] In a donation experiment, a letter about one named child raised $2.38 on average, against $1.14 for statistics about the same crisis.[3]

The gap. Of 59 about and history pages, 68% stated a founding date, but only 42% described what happened at the start, 34% named a founder and 15% put a customer in the origin story. On those pages, "we", "our" and "us" appeared eight times as often as "you" and "your".

The answer. Climb the Story Ladder: a date, a person, an event, a customer, and one line people can repeat. Then turn the camera around so the customer is the hero and the company is the guide.

The essay in 2 minutes
  1. Finding 12,706%

    A story multiplied the price of junk by about 28.

    Significant Objects bought 100 objects for $128.74 and sold them with invented stories for $3,612.51, a 2,706% increase. A second 100 rose 2,860%.[2]

  2. Finding 2$2.38

    One named person raised twice what statistics raised.

    In Small, Loewenstein and Slovic's Study 3 (159 participants), donations averaged $2.38 for an identifiable child, $1.14 for statistics, and $1.43 when the child came with the statistics.[3] A 2024 replication with hypothetical donations did not find the effect, so treat it with care.[4]

  3. Finding 314 in 100

    Stories that are forgotten fast do not spread.

    In Shiller's version of the epidemic model, a story that is retold faster than it is forgotten reaches almost everyone. In our rerun of the same model, making it ten times easier to forget left it reaching about 14 people in 100.[1]

  4. Finding 415%

    Industrial about pages give the date and skip the story.

    Of 59 pages, 68% stated a founding date, 42% described an origin event, 34% named a founder and 15% named the first customer or customer group.

  5. Finding 51,828

    The customer is almost absent.

    On the 59 about pages, company words (we, our, us) appeared 1,828 times and reader words (you, your) 230 times. 56 of 59 pages used more company words than reader words.

Chapter 1 · 1 min

What brand storytelling is

A brand story is not the company history. History lists what happened to the business. A story picks one moment, puts a person in it, and ends with something a customer could repeat at dinner.

Chapter 2 · 3 min

The evidence, part 1: a story changes what something is worth

The Significant Objects experiment

In 2009 the journalist Rob Walker and Joshua Glenn ran a project to test one hypothesis: "Narrative transforms insignificant objects into significant ones."[2] They bought cheap objects from thrift stores and yard sales, asked writers to invent a back story for each, and listed the objects on eBay with the story as the description. Nothing about the objects changed. Only the story did.

The project published its own totals. We recomputed the averages and the markup from them.

The project reported the increase as 2,706% for volume 1 and 2,860% for volumes 2 and 3, and our arithmetic matches both. Across all 200 objects, $263.63 of objects sold for $7,605.44, about 28.9 times what they cost.

The project also published the final eBay price of each of the first 100 objects. They add up to exactly the $3,612.51 total, so we used them to see whether a few big sales drove the result.

The lift was broad, not a few lucky auctions. The median object sold for $26.00, roughly 20 times the average purchase price of $1.29.

Be fair about the limits. This was a project by writers, not a controlled experiment. There was no group of the same objects sold without stories, and the writers' names and the press attention may have drawn buyers on their own. The project's curators noted themselves that early auctions sold for less before word spread. Still, the pattern lines up with what brand equity means in practice: meaning that makes people pay more for an object that looks the same.

One person against the statistics

A controlled test of a related idea comes from D. A. Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic, published in Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes in 2007.[3] People on a university campus in Pennsylvania were paid $5 for an unrelated survey and then given a letter from the charity Save the Children, with the chance to donate any of the five dollars. In Study 3, 159 people took part, and the letter took one of three forms.

The story of one person raised about twice what the statistics did, and adding the statistics pulled giving back down: the authors found no difference between the statistics-only and combined groups. The numbers diluted the story.

This finding needs a warning. In 2024, Maier, Wong and Feldman ran a pre-registered replication of Studies 1 and 3 with hypothetical rather than real donations and "found no empirical support for the identifiable victim effect".[4] They note that their design differed from the original, which used real money. Read Figure 3 as one strong result that is now contested, not as a law.

Neither study involved B2B buyers. Together they show that stories can change value judgments, not that any given brand story will.

Chapter 3 · 2 min

The evidence, part 2: how stories spread and fade

Shiller's address borrowed a 1927 model of disease epidemics, the Kermack-McKendrick model, and applied it to stories.[1] People who have not heard the story can catch it from those who are spreading it. Spreaders stop, through forgetting, at a steady rate. In Shiller's words, the contagion rate is the fraction of encounters that "effectively convinces the susceptible enough of the story to spread it further", and the removal rate "might be described as the rate of forgetting".

His Figure 2 shows one example: 100 people, one initial spreader, a contagion rate of 0.005 and a forgetting rate of 0.05. He shows that the story reaches almost everyone when the ratio of the two is high enough, and that by his definition it "goes viral" when that ratio is above 0.014. We reran his example and added two variations of our own with the same model.

By time 200, Shiller's example had reached 100 of 100 people and the half-as-retold story 99.3. The story that was forgotten ten times faster never took off. It reached about 13.5 people in total and died out.

The model is a metaphor, but a practical one. Whether a story spreads depends on how often people pass it on and how fast they forget it. A person and an event are easier to remember than "founded in 1955 and committed to excellence". A line a customer can say in one breath is easier to pass on.

Two founding stories that spread

The best-known company stories are small and specific. HP's archive brochure records that Bill Hewlett and Dave Packard began part-time work in a garage at 367 Addison Avenue in Palo Alto with $538 in working capital, including the value of a used drill press, that the company was founded in 1939 and named with a coin toss, and that the garage became California Historical Landmark No. 976 in 1987.[5] Nike's own account says that in 1971 Bill Bowerman poured liquid urethane into his wife Barbara's waffle iron, which glued shut for good, and that by 1975 orders for the resulting Waffle Trainer had topped 100,000 units.[6] Neither story lists features. Each gives a listener a person, an object and a moment.

Chapter 4 · 4 min

Original research: what do industrial about pages say about their origins?

The evidence above describes stories in general. We wanted to see how companies in our own sector tell theirs. On 3 November 2026 we read the about and history pages of the 91 US mid-cap manufacturing and supply-chain companies we also studied in our essay on brand positioning: every company in the S&P MidCap 400 and S&P SmallCap 600 lists in eleven manufacturing and freight sub-industries.

Who we could measure

67 of 91 homepages returned a readable page to our research script. From those homepages we followed links to an about page and, where one existed, a separate history page. 59 companies had at least one such page we could read: 57 about pages and 21 history pages. Seven homepages linked to neither, and one about page refused our request. Founding years for the whole sample came from Wikidata.

Finding 1: these are old companies

The median founding year was 1944, which makes the median company 82 years old. 30 of the 77 (39%) were founded in 1926 or earlier and are at least a century old. These companies are not short of material.

Finding 2: the date is there, the story often is not

We read each about and history page and recorded four story elements, using written rules. A founding date counted if the page gave a year or decade as the start of the company or its business. A founder counted if a person was named as the one who founded or started it. An origin event counted if the page described a concrete first act or circumstance: a first product, an invention, a purchase, a problem, or what the founders did, beyond the type of company. A customer counted if that origin account named who the first product or service was for.

Only 7 of the 59 (12%) had all four elements. 18 (31%) had none of them. The most common pattern was a bare date: "Founded in 1955", "Since 1917", "Founded in 1944". A date tells a reader that a company is old. It does not give them anything to retell.

The pages that did tell a story were memorable because they were specific. Worthington's history says that in 1955 "a young steel salesman named John H. McConnell" borrowed "$600 against his 1952 Oldsmobile" to buy his first load of steel. Moog's says that in 1951 three founders "pooled $3,000 and opened Moog Valve". Marten Transport says it "started it all in 1946 with a single milk truck in rural Wisconsin".

The rarest element was the customer. Donaldson's history is one of the nine that has one: in 1915, Frank Donaldson Sr., then a tractor salesman, "visited a customer whose tractor kept breaking down in his dusty field" and fashioned an air cleaner to protect the engine. That is a complete brand story in two sentences: a customer with a problem, a guide with a fix, and a product line that still exists. Powell's page does something similar in one line: it was founded in 1947 "as a metal-working shop to support the local Houston, Texas petrochemical facilities".

A smaller check points the same way. Wikidata names a founder for 8 of the 59 companies, and 4 of those 8 pages name that founder.

Finding 3: about pages talk about "we", not "you"

We counted first-person company words (we, our, us, ours) and second-person reader words (you, your, yours) on the same pages, and on the homepages for comparison.

On about pages, company words outnumbered reader words by 1,828 to 230, almost eight to one, and 56 of 59 pages used more company words than reader words. Homepages ran a little over two to one. An about page is about the company, but the customer appeared only about five times per 1,000 words.

Limits of our study

  • One day and one sample of US-listed companies, many of them groups formed by mergers, which makes a single origin story harder to tell.
  • We read only pages linked from the homepage, in raw HTML without JavaScript. A company may tell its story elsewhere, in video or on a subsidiary's site.
  • One coder applied the rules. The coding sheet, with the quoted evidence for every positive, is published with this essay.
  • We measured what pages say, not whether stories win business.

A framework: the Story Ladder

Each rung adds something a listener can hold on to. A date proves age. A person and an event make it memorable. A customer makes it about the reader. A line makes it repeatable.

Chapter 6 · 2 min

How to write a brand story: the five-part method

The Story Ladder fixes the origin story. The everyday brand story, on the homepage, in sales calls and in proposals, puts the customer at the centre. It has five parts.

Step 1: Make the customer the hero

Write one sentence: [Customer] wants [result], but [problem] keeps getting in the way. "Manufacturers" is too broad. "Purchasing managers at mid-size equipment makers who need parts on the promised date" is a hero. Name the feeling under the problem too: they are the one who explains a stopped line to the plant director.

Step 2: Make your business the guide

The guide shows up with understanding and a plan of three or four plain steps. Your origin story belongs here: Donaldson's founder fixing a customer's tractor is a guide story.

Step 3: Name the stakes

Say what happens if nothing changes and what changes if it does. "You'll keep paying for rush freight and explaining late builds" is a stake. "Transform your supply chain" is not.

Step 4: Bring the proof, but don't drown the story

Proof makes people believe you: a named reference, a delivery record, a certification, a short case study. The Brave New case studies show one way to structure it. Note the warning in Figure 3: adding statistics to a story reduced giving. Put proof next to the story, not inside it.

Step 5: Write one line people can repeat

Boil it down to a sentence a customer could say to a friend: "They're the shop that gives you a real delivery date and then hits it." Test it with the question "They're the people who ___?"

A worked example

This case is made up, but it's typical. Say a 40-person parts manufacturer makes machined components for packaging-equipment builders.

  • Hero: purchasing managers whose current suppliers keep slipping.
  • Guide: the shop. Its founder was a buyer at an equipment maker before starting it in a rented bay with one lathe (origin story), and it sends every customer a weekly order-tracking report (authority).
  • Stakes: without help, rush freight and late builds. With help, plans that hold.
  • Proof: three named references and an on-time record a buyer can check.
  • Line: "They're the shop that gives you a real delivery date and then hits it."

Every part could be checked by calling a customer, so the short version stays true when people retell it.

Chapter 7 · 1 min

Where to tell your brand story

Tell it wherever a customer meets you, at different lengths, with the same hero, stakes and line: the homepage headline, the about page, sales calls and proposals, reviews and social posts. A story told the same way a hundred times beats a brilliant one told once. That is also the root of good positioning. And someone has to own it. The Hive's review of The Founder argues that the McDonald brothers built the better system, but Ray Kroc understood the name and the story.

Chapter 8 · 1 min

What this means for leaders

For a manufacturer or a logistics company, the story is already in the building: in the archive, in the founder's first order, in the customer whose problem started a product line. Our data says most mid-cap industrial companies publish the date and leave the rest in the drawer.

That matters most when a company expands. A buyer in a new country or industry has never heard of you, and most buyers are not buying today. When they are, they pick from the names that come to mind, which is why a specific story builds mental availability better than a list of dates. One story in one market is manageable. The same story in five markets and three languages is not. We build AI flows that take one true story and adapt its length, language and examples for each market and buyer, so a business can go broad without hiring at the same rate. The hero, the stakes and the repeatable line stay fixed.

Chapter 9 · 1 min

Common mistakes

  • Publishing a date instead of a story. "Founded in 1955" was the most common pattern in our scan. It proves age, not character.
  • Making the company the hero. On industrial about pages, company words outnumbered reader words almost eight to one.
  • Leaving out the first customer. Only 15% of the pages we read named who the business was first built for.
  • Burying the story in statistics. In Small et al., adding numbers to a story lowered giving.
  • Stretching the truth. People shorten stories as they retell them. A shaky origin story becomes a myth you have to defend.

Chapter 10 · 1 min

Methodology

Sample and fetch. All 91 companies on Wikipedia's S&P MidCap 400 and S&P SmallCap 600 constituent pages in eleven GICS Industrials manufacturing and freight sub-industries, with websites from Wikidata (P856), five corrected by hand. On 3 November 2026 a script requested each homepage once as "BraveNewResearch/1.0" with a link to brave-new.com, without JavaScript and without retrying blocked requests. 67 returned a readable page. From each, a script picked links to a history page (history, heritage, story, legacy, timeline) and an about page (about, who we are, company), and fetched them once. Where the automatic pick chose the homepage, a product or awards page or a duplicate, we chose the matching link by hand from the links on that company's homepage (21 companies). 59 companies had at least one readable page.

Coding and counts. Story elements were coded by hand using the rules in Finding 2; mergers, spin-offs and buyouts did not count as origin events. Word counts matched whole words on pages with at least 100 visible words. Founding years are the earliest Wikidata inception date (P571), available for 77 of 91.

Published data. Figures 1 and 2 are recomputed from the Significant Objects project's published totals and price table. Figure 4 solves the Kermack-McKendrick model with Shiller's published parameters in steps of 0.01 time units; the two variations are ours.

Chapter 11 · 1 min

Frequently asked questions

What is brand storytelling?

Brand storytelling is the practice of explaining a business as a story instead of a list of features or dates. The customer is the hero with a problem, the business is the guide with a plan, and the story ends with the result the customer wants. Done well, it gives people one sentence they can repeat to someone else.

Does storytelling change what people pay?

There is good evidence that it can. In the Significant Objects project, thrift-store items bought for an average of $1.29 sold for about $36 on eBay with invented stories, roughly 28 times their cost. That project had no control group, and lab evidence on stories and giving is mixed, so treat stories as a strong lever, not a guarantee.

What should an about page include?

Our scan of 59 industrial about pages suggests four elements: a founding date, a named founder, a concrete origin event, and the first customer or customer problem. Most pages had the date. Only 15% named a customer in the origin story. Add the person and the event first, then the customer, and keep every claim checkable.

Should a brand story be about the founder?

The founder can be the guide, but the customer should be the hero. The strongest origin stories in our scan showed a founder solving a specific customer's problem, such as a salesman fixing a customer's tractor in a dusty field. That kind of story explains why you are trustworthy and keeps the buyer at the centre.

Your next step

This week, open your own about page and score it against the ladder: date, person, event, customer, line. Then call two of your happiest customers and ask one question: "How would you describe us to a friend?" Write down their exact words. That sentence is your first draft of rung 5, and the gap between their words and your about page is your rewrite plan.
