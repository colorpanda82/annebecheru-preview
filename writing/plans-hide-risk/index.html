Pre-mortem: why plans hide risk, and what 229 forecasts reveal

By Anne Becheru, founder of Brave New Media.

Published 3 Nov 2026 · Updated 9 Nov 202619 min read

Start chapter 1 →Key findings ↓

A pre-mortem is a planning exercise in which a team imagines that its project has already failed and works backwards to explain why, before any money is committed. The psychologist Gary Klein described it in the Harvard Business Review in September 2007. In his version, team members assume that the project they are planning has just failed.[1] A post-mortem asks what killed the patient. A pre-mortem asks the same question while the patient can still be saved.

This essay brings together three bodies of evidence on why plans need one. The first is psychology research on the planning fallacy, the habit of underestimating time and cost. The second is Oxford research on hundreds of real projects, from rail lines to IT systems, that ran over budget. The third is our own study of 229 quarterly forecasts made by US professional forecasters since 1968, compared with what the economy did. We close with a framework, the Four Views of a Plan, and a 30-minute pre-mortem any team can run this week.

Idea in brief

The problem. A plan describes one future, the one its authors expect. In a study of 258 transport projects, actual costs were higher than estimated in 86% of cases.[3] Plans are not neutral forecasts. They lean towards hope.

Why it happens. People plan from the inside: the tasks, the team, the timeline in front of them. Even when students were asked to predict their finish date "if everything went as poorly as it possibly could," the average guess was still too early.[2]

The gap. In our study of the Survey of Professional Forecasters, the median one-year forecast for US growth missed by more than one percentage point in 48% of 229 surveys. At the start of seven of the last eight recessions, the median forecast still showed growth. Yet in the same surveys the forecasters put the chance of a falling quarter at between 15% and 51%. The risk was written down. It just was not in the headline number.

The answer. Look at a plan from four sides: the inside view, the outside view (what happened to similar projects), the range of outcomes, and the failure view (the pre-mortem). Then give every top risk an owner and an early warning sign.

Key findings

The essay in 2 minutes
  1. Finding 155.5 days

    Even worst-case plans are too hopeful.

    Students predicted their thesis would take 33.9 days. It took 55.5. Their "everything goes wrong" estimate, 48.6 days, was still short, and only 48.7% finished by it.[2]

  2. Finding 227.6%

    Cost overruns are the norm.

    Across 258 transport projects in 20 nations, costs averaged 27.6% over estimate. In 1,471 IT projects, one in six overran by 200% on average.[3][6]

  3. Finding 31.35 pts

    Expert forecasts miss by a lot, often.

    Across 229 quarterly surveys from 1968 to 2025, the median forecast of US growth over the next four quarters missed the outcome by 1.35 percentage points on average. A quarter of forecasts missed by more than 2 points.[8][9]

  4. Finding 47 of 8

    Point forecasts hide the risk that probabilities show.

    At the start of each recession since 1970, forecasters gave a falling quarter odds of 15% to 51%, yet the median growth forecast was positive in 7 of 8 cases.[8]

  5. Finding 517.4%

    Four quarters before each recession began, forecasters put the chance of a decline in that quarter at 10% to 30%. Their typical reading across all surveys was 17.4%.[8]

Chapter 1 · 1 min

What a pre-mortem is

A pre-mortem is a structured meeting, held before a decision is final, that treats failure as a fact and asks the team to explain it. A risk register lists the risks a team already knows. Reference class forecasting fixes a plan's numbers by starting from how similar projects turned out. A post-mortem comes too late to help the project it studies. The pre-mortem finds the risks nobody has written down yet, because nobody felt free to.

Chapter 2 · 1 min

The evidence, part 1: the planning fallacy

The best known test of the planning fallacy comes from Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 1994.[2] They telephoned 37 psychology students in the final semester of an honours thesis course at the University of Waterloo. Each student gave three predictions: a best guess, a guess "if everything went as well as it possibly could," and a guess "if everything went as poorly as it possibly could." The course coordinator then recorded when each thesis was handed in.

The best guess averaged 33.9 days. The real average was 55.5, and fewer than a third of students (29.7%) finished by the date they named. The striking row is the pessimistic one. Asked to imagine everything going as badly as possible, students still came in a week short on average, and fewer than half (48.7%) beat even that date.[2]

This is the core of why plans hide risk. Planners are not lying or careless. They build the future from the parts they can see, and the rest never reaches the page. Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow explains the mechanism at book length, and the Hive's entry on System 1 and System 2 covers the fast, confident thinking behind it.

Limits of this evidence

  • It is one study of 33 students (four had not finished when records stopped), not managers.
  • A thesis is a solo task. Business plans involve teams, suppliers and buyers, which adds more ways to be late, not fewer.

Chapter 3 · 2 min

The evidence, part 2: what real projects cost

The Oxford professor Bent Flyvbjerg and colleagues have collected data on how real projects compare with their approved estimates. Their 2002 paper in the Journal of the American Planning Association covered 258 transport projects worth $90 billion, in 20 nations on five continents.[3]

For a randomly chosen project in the sample, the chance that actual costs exceeded the estimate was 86%. The authors found that cost underestimation appeared in all 20 nations studied.[3] In a 2014 overview, Flyvbjerg summed up the record for very large projects: nine out of ten have cost overruns, and overruns have stayed high and constant for the 70 years for which comparable data exist.[4]

The averages are only the start. The same overview lists some of the most famous overruns.

The Channel Tunnel row carries a lesson about how plans talk about risk. At its public offering, Eurotunnel told investors that 10% "would be a reasonable allowance for the possible impact of unforeseen circumstances on construction costs." Construction costs went 80% over budget, and financing costs 140%.[4] The plan did mention risk. It priced it at an eighth of what arrived.

The fat tail in IT projects

For most companies, the closest thing to a megaproject is a new IT system. Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier studied 1,471 IT projects for a 2011 Harvard Business Review article. The average cost overrun was 27%. But one project in six was what they called a black swan, with a cost overrun of 200% on average and a schedule overrun of almost 70%.[6]

Two of their examples come from supply chains. At Levi Strauss, an ERP migration with a proposed budget under $5 million left the company unable to fill orders, forced it to close its three US distribution centres for a week, and ended in a $192.5 million charge against earnings in the second quarter of 2008. At Hershey, a new order-taking and fulfilment system stopped the company shipping $100 million worth of candy in time for Halloween, and quarterly earnings fell 18.6%.[6] Neither failure was in the plan. Both were the kind of thing a warehouse supervisor might have named in a pre-mortem.

Limits of this evidence

  • The transport and IT samples are mostly large public projects (92% of the IT sample was public agencies, 83% US-based).[6] A 40-person company's projects are smaller.
  • Figure 3 shows the extreme tail, not the typical project.

Chapter 4 · 1 min

The evidence, part 3: how much to add

Flyvbjerg's answer to the planning fallacy is reference class forecasting, built on the "outside view" described by Kahneman and Tversky. Lovallo and Kahneman (2003) traced the planning fallacy to the inside view: focusing on the parts of one project rather than the outcomes of similar projects already finished.[5] In the summer of 2004, the UK Department for Transport and HM Treasury decided to use reference class forecasting in the appraisal of large transport projects.[5]

The method turns past overruns into an uplift: the percentage to add to a budget for a chosen chance of staying within it.

Read across a row and you see the price of certainty. A road scheme that wants even odds of staying in budget adds 15%. One that wants to be 80% sure adds 32%. In Flyvbjerg's worked example, a motorway budget of £100 million, planned for an 80% chance of staying within it, becomes £132 million.[5] The plan did not change. Only the honesty about its range did.

Chapter 5 · 3 min

Original research: how often do expert forecasts miss?

The studies above describe projects. We wanted to test the same idea on some of the most closely watched forecasts there are: professional forecasts of the economy, which demand, pricing and hiring plans quietly depend on. So on 3 November 2026 we compared every quarterly Survey of Professional Forecasters since 1968 with what the US economy then did.

The Survey of Professional Forecasters is run by the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia. Each quarter, it asks a panel of professional forecasters for their view of real GDP growth for the current quarter and the next four, and for the probability that real GDP will fall in each of those quarters.[8] We took the median growth forecast for the four quarters starting with the survey quarter and compared it with actual growth over the same four quarters, using the latest figures published by the Philadelphia Fed's Real-Time Data Research Center.[9] The full method is at the end of this essay.

Finding 1: half of forecasts miss by more than a point

On average, forecasts were close: a mean forecast of 2.67% growth against a mean outcome of 2.74%. The average hides the spread. The typical miss was 1.35 percentage points, 48% of surveys missed by more than a point, and a quarter (24.9%) missed by more than two. In an economy that grows around 2.7% a year, a two-point miss is the difference between a good year and a flat one.

The misses were not evenly spread. In the 57 surveys whose four-quarter window included a recession, outcomes fell short of the forecast 63.2% of the time, by 1.18 points on average, and the typical miss doubled to 2.15 points. In the 172 surveys that covered only expansion, the typical miss was 1.08 points and outcomes beat the forecast more often than not.

Finding 2: the risk was in the probability, not the plan

This is the clearest picture we know of how a plan hides risk. In seven of the eight surveys taken as a recession was starting, the median forecast still showed growth over the next year. Growth over that year then turned out negative in seven of the eight, though only by 0.04 points in 1980.

But the forecasters were not blind. In the same surveys, the average probability they gave to a falling quarter was between 29% and 51% in seven of eight cases, against an average of 19.3% across all 232 surveys. The risk was on the form. It simply did not survive the trip into the single number that a budget or a sales plan would use. The exception, 2020, makes the point from the other side: responses were due by mid-February 2020, before the pandemic reached the US economy, and no plan could have priced it.

Finding 3: a year out, recessions look like ordinary years

Most annual plans are written about a year ahead. A year before each recession, the forecasters' odds of a decline in the quarter it would start sat between 10% and 30%, close to their typical reading of 17.4%. From twelve months out, the year a recession arrives looks much like any other year. If the best-resourced forecasters in the US miss by more than a point half the time, a company's sales forecast will miss too. A plan written a year ahead has to carry its own range, and ask what the team would do at the bottom of it. That is the question a pre-mortem forces.

Chapter 6 · 1 min

A framework: the Four Views of a Plan

The evidence points to four ways of looking at any plan. Each catches something the others miss. Most plans only ever get the first.

ViewQuestion it answersEvidenceWhat to produce
1. Inside viewWhat will it take if things go as we expect?Useful for organising work, but biased: even worst-case student estimates fell short.[2]Tasks, owners, timeline, budget
2. Outside viewHow did similar projects turn out?Costs exceeded estimates in 86% of 258 projects; UK appraisal uses uplifts of 15% to 57%.[3][5]A reference class and an uplift on time and cost
3. Range viewHow wide is the spread of outcomes, and what is in the tail?One IT project in six overran by 200%; expert growth forecasts missed by over a point in 48% of surveys.[6]A low case, a base case and a high case, with the low case funded
4. Failure viewIt failed. Why?Professional forecasters wrote recession risk on the form but not into the number.A pre-mortem list: top risks, owners, early warning signs

Run them in order. The outside view corrects the plan's numbers, the range view sets the cushion, and the failure view finds the specific risks no reference class will show, such as the one supplier everyone knows is fragile.

Chapter 7 · 2 min

How to run a 30-minute pre-mortem

This is the method we recommend for a growing business. It works with two people or twelve.

Step 1: Name the plan and the date (3 minutes)

Name one specific plan: a product launch, a new distributor, a price change, an ERP switch, an entry into a new country. Pick the date by which you would know whether it worked, usually 6 to 12 months out. Write both where everyone can see them.

Step 2: Announce the failure (2 minutes)

Say it plainly: "It's next October. This launch failed. It cost us money." Do not soften it to "underperformed". Klein's design depends on the team treating failure as something that has already happened.[1]

Step 3: Write alone, in silence (8 minutes)

Each person writes their own list of reasons. No talking, because the first voice shapes the rest. Ask for specifics: "the casting supplier missed the March delivery" beats "operations problems".

Tip: The leader writes too, and shares last. If the founder's reasons come first, the team stops at those.

Step 4: Share one reason at a time (10 minutes)

Go round the room, one reason per person per turn, until the lists run out. Record every reason. Group similar ones. No debate yet.

Step 5: Pick three, and give each an owner and a signal (7 minutes)

Vote on the three reasons that are most likely or would hurt most. For each, agree who owns it and what early sign would show it starting. Then change the plan where you can.

This case is made up, but it is typical. Say a 60-person food-packaging manufacturer plans to start selling through distributors in a second country. Its pre-mortem might end like this:

Risk foundOwnerEarly warning signPlan change
Launch slips because the one approved casting supplier is lateOperations leadFewer than 2 sample batches confirmed by week 6Qualify a second supplier before launch
First distributors order once and stopSales leadFewer than half reorder within 90 daysAdd a technical onboarding call for each distributor
A quality complaint spreads before we can respondMarketing leadAny public complaint or returned batchWrite a response plan before launch

The last row matters more than most teams expect. The Hive explains reputation risk, and the Tylenol case of 1982 shows how an event outside any plan can land on a company. A pre-mortem cannot predict that kind of event. It can settle in advance who speaks and what happens first.

Then fix the numbers

Pull the planned and actual time and cost for your last five similar projects, start from their typical overrun, and only then adjust for what is truly different. Gathering that history by hand is slow, which is why most teams skip it. It is the kind of job we build AI flows for: pulling past quotes, lead times and project records into one view, so a team can check a new plan against its own history before taking an idea to more markets and more buyers.

Chapter 8 · 1 min

What this means for leaders

For a manufacturer or a supply-chain business, the riskiest plans are usually growth plans: a new market, a new channel, a new system to support more customers. They are new to the team, so the inside view is weakest. They depend on distributors, suppliers and regulators who are not in the planning meeting. And they are written about a year out, the horizon at which, in our data, a coming downturn looked like any other year.

The evidence does not say "do not expand". Several projects in Figure 3, such as the Sydney Opera House, are now landmarks. It says that a growth plan should carry its range openly, start its numbers from what similar moves cost, and be put through a pre-mortem by the people closest to the operation. Companies that do this can move into more markets with fewer surprises, because the surprises were named first.

Chapter 9 · 1 min

Common mistakes

  • Running the pre-mortem after the contract is signed. Once money is spent, nobody says what they think. Hold it before the commitment.
  • Talking before writing, or softening the failure. The loudest voice sets the list, and "it underperformed" gets mild answers.
  • Blaming only the outside world. The economy and competitors are easy targets. Push for reasons inside your control: hiring, cash, suppliers, timing.
  • Ending with a list. A risk without an owner and an early warning sign changes nothing.
  • Trusting the average. A 27% average overrun is survivable. The one-in-six 200% overrun is the kind that has brought down whole companies, such as Kmart.[6]
  • Treating a point forecast as the plan. In our data, the risk was in the probability column, not the headline number.

The Hive's entry on loss aversion explains part of why this is hard: imagining a loss is uncomfortable, and that discomfort is what makes the exercise useful.

Chapter 10 · 1 min

Methodology

Data. The Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia's Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF): the file of median forecasts for real GDP growth (quarter-on-quarter, annualised, for the survey quarter and the next four) and the file of mean probabilities of a decline in real GDP (RECESS1 to RECESS5).[8] Actual growth came from the Philadelphia Fed Real-Time Data Research Center file for real GNP/GDP (ROUTPUT), "most recent" release, last updated 30 October 2026.[9] Recession dates came from the NBER-based indicator USREC on FRED.[10] All files were downloaded on 3 November 2026 with a script that identified itself as "BraveNewResearch/1.0".

Measures. The forecast is the median growth forecast for the survey quarter and the next three quarters, compounded into four-quarter growth. The actual is the same four quarters compounded from the latest data. A survey window counts as containing a recession if any of its quarters includes an NBER recession month. For Figure 6 we used the survey taken in the first quarter of each recession and the mean probability of a decline in the following quarter, which the Philadelphia Fed calls the Anxious Index. For Figure 7 we used the survey four quarters earlier and the probability of a decline in the fourth quarter after the survey.

Denominators. 229 surveys from 1968 Q4 to 2025 Q4 had both a forecast and a full four quarters of actual data. 57 windows contained a recession and 172 did not. Probability data were available for 232 surveys at the one-quarter horizon and 228 at the four-quarter horizon. Eight recessions began after the survey started, from 1970 to 2020.

Robustness. Using the BEA's third estimate instead of the latest data (224 surveys), the average miss was 1.28 points and 46.0% of surveys missed by more than a point.

Limits. Before 1992 the survey asked about real GNP, and later data revisions change the "actual" figures. We measured the median forecaster and the whole US economy, not any company's sales. Scripts and data are published with this essay.

Chapter 11 · 1 min

Frequently asked questions

What is a pre-mortem in business?

A pre-mortem is a short team meeting held before a project starts. The leader tells the team the project has already failed, and each person writes down why. The group shares the reasons, picks the most serious, and changes the plan. Gary Klein described the method in Harvard Business Review in September 2007.

What is the difference between a pre-mortem and a post-mortem?

A post-mortem happens after a project ends and asks what went wrong. A pre-mortem happens before it starts and asks what will have gone wrong, as if failure already happened. The questions are similar. The timing is the point: a pre-mortem gives you the lessons while you can still act on them.

What is the planning fallacy?

The planning fallacy is the tendency to believe your own project will go to plan while knowing that most similar projects ran late or over budget. Kahneman and Tversky named it in 1979. In one study, students predicted their thesis would take 33.9 days. It took 55.5, and even their worst-case estimate was too short.

How much should I add to a project budget for risk?

Start from how similar projects turned out. UK transport appraisal adds 15% to road budgets for even odds of staying within them, and 32% for 80% confidence. Rail adds 40% and 57%. For your own business, use your last five comparable projects as the reference class and add their typical overrun.

How accurate are economic forecasts for business planning?

Less accurate than most plans assume. In our study of 229 quarterly surveys of US professional forecasters since 1968, the median one-year growth forecast missed by more than one percentage point 48% of the time. At the start of seven of the last eight recessions, the median forecast still showed growth.

Your next step

The investor Howard Marks likes to quote the London Business School professor Elroy Dimson: "Risk means more things can happen than will happen." Marks adds that people who rely heavily on forecasts "seem to think there's only one possibility."[7] A plan is that kind of forecast.

This week, pick the next decision that will cost real money. Book 30 minutes with the three people closest to it, tell them it failed, and give them eight quiet minutes to write why. Then pull the planned and actual numbers for your last five similar projects. Leave with three risks, three owners, three warning signs and one honest uplift.
