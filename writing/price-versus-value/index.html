Value-based pricing: the data on price vs. value, and how 91 industrial websites handle price

Brave New MediaPublished 3 November 202621 min read

Start chapter 1 →Key findings ↓

Value-based pricing is the practice of setting a price from what a product or service is worth to the buyer, rather than from what it costs the seller to provide. Cost still sets the floor, but the buyer's result sets the ceiling. The idea behind it is old. Warren Buffett credits his teacher Benjamin Graham with the line "Price is what you pay; value is what you get," which he repeated in his 2008 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders.[1]

This essay brings together four bodies of evidence. The first is the profit arithmetic of price, from a widely cited McKinsey analysis of the average S&P 1500 company and from current US industry margins published by NYU Stern's Aswath Damodaran. The second is evidence on where price leaks between the list and the bank account. The third is a long record of pricing power from one small business, See's Candies, reported by its owner. The fourth is our own scan of 91 US mid-cap manufacturing and supply-chain companies, which shows how industrial firms handle price, quotes and value on their public websites. We close with a framework, the Price-Value Matrix, and a five-step method any marketing or sales team can use.

Idea in brief

The problem. Price is the strongest profit lever most companies have. At an average S&P 1500 company, a 1% price rise with steady volume lifts operating profit by 8%, and a 5% price cut needs an 18.7% rise in volume just to break even.[2] Yet most firms set price by looking at their own costs, then give part of it away in discounts.

Why it happens. Buyers cannot measure value directly. They judge it by comparison and by the evidence a seller puts in front of them. When that evidence is missing, the conversation falls back to the only number on the table: the price.

The gap (our data). Among 63 industrial homepages we could read, 95% mentioned or linked to investors, 11% offered a way to request a quote, and none showed a product price. Of 19 homepages that showed any dollar figure, 9 used it for financial results. Only one used a dollar figure to show what a customer saves.

The answer. Price from value, then prove the value where buyers look. Work through the Price-Value Matrix: make the value visible, then protect the price you set.

Chapter 1 · 1 min

What value-based pricing is

Value-based pricing asks one question first: what is this worth to the buyer? A cost-plus price asks a different one: what did this cost us, and what margin do we want on top? Both methods produce a number. Only one of them looks at the person paying it.

Take a made-up but typical case: a part that costs $80 to make and sells for $100 on a cost-plus basis. If it prevents a line stoppage worth $2,000 to the buyer, the supplier has left most of the value on the table.

Terms used in this essay

TermMeaning
Value-based pricingSetting price from the buyer's result: money saved, money made, risk avoided or time freed.
Cost-plus pricingSetting price as cost plus a target margin.
Pricing powerThe ability to raise price without losing business to a competitor. Buffett called it "the single most important decision in evaluating a business."[5]
List priceThe published or quoted price before any discount.
Pocket priceWhat the seller keeps from a transaction after every discount, rebate, allowance and free service, on or off the invoice.[2]
Price waterfallThe chart of every step from list price down to pocket price.
Reference priceWhatever the buyer compares your price with: a rival, your old price, doing it in-house, or doing nothing.
Operating marginOperating profit as a share of revenue. It sets how strongly price changes move profit.

Cost does not disappear in value-based pricing. Cost is the floor. Value is the ceiling. The price belongs between them, and for most firms we see it sits closer to the floor than it should.

Chapter 2 · 2 min

The evidence, part 1: price is the strongest profit lever

The best known figure on this comes from "The power of pricing," a 2003 McKinsey Quarterly article by Michael Marn, Eric Roegner and Craig Zawada.[2] The authors took the income statement of an average company in the S&P 1500 and asked what a 1% change in each lever would do to operating profit.

The first two rows are symmetrical: a 1% price rise adds 8% to operating profit and a 1% cut removes the same 8%. The authors note that the price effect was nearly 50% greater than a 1% cut in variable costs such as materials and direct labour, and more than three times greater than a 1% rise in volume.[2]

The third row is the one we show sales teams: to make up for a 5% price cut, the average company would need to sell 18.7% more just to stand still.[2]

Why the effect is bigger in thin-margin industries

The 8% figure is an average. The effect depends on margin, and the arithmetic is simple. If volume and costs stay the same, a 1% price rise adds 1% of revenue to operating profit. Divide that by the operating margin and you get the percentage lift in profit. A company with a 10% margin gets a 10% lift. A company with a 4% margin gets a 25% lift.

We applied that arithmetic to the US industry margins Aswath Damodaran publishes at NYU Stern, using data as of January 2026.[3]

Two things stand out. First, the total-market result (7.8%) lands almost exactly where McKinsey's 2003 average did (8%), more than two decades later. Second, the thin-margin, commodity-like industries at the top of the table, such as basic chemicals, steel and auto parts, are where a small price gain does the most good. A parts supplier with a 6% margin that holds 1% more on price raises its operating profit by about a sixth.

Tip: Run this for your own business. Divide 1% by your operating margin. That is what one point of price is worth to you, and it tells you how much a routine 3% discount really costs.

Limits of this evidence

  • Both figures assume volume holds. In real markets, a price rise loses some buyers, and the question is how many you can lose and still come out ahead.
  • Damodaran's margins are industry aggregates for listed US companies. Your margin is the one that matters.
  • The McKinsey analysis is from 2003. We cite it because the arithmetic has not changed, and our 2026 calculation gives nearly the same answer.

Chapter 3 · 1 min

The evidence, part 2: where price leaks

Setting a good list price is half the work. The other half is keeping it. The same McKinsey article describes a global lighting supplier that looked at every step between its list price and the money it kept.[2]

Every bulb had a standard list price. Discounts on the invoice pushed the average invoice price 32.8% below it. Then came the items nobody saw on the invoice, including cash discounts for prompt payment, the cost of carrying receivables, cooperative advertising allowances and rebates based on each distributor's annual volume. Those took another 16.3 percentage points, leaving an average pocket price of about half the list price.[2] The spread across customers was wider still. Some bulbs sold at a pocket price below 30% of list and others at 90% or more.[2]

The article reports what happened when companies managed this waterfall on purpose. In one case, the average pocket price rose 3.6% in the first year and operating profit rose 51%. In another, a 4% rise in average pocket margin lifted operating profit by 60% within a year.[2]

This is value-based pricing seen from the other side. A company that cannot show buyers what its product is worth will usually pay for that gap in discounts, and the discounts rarely show up in one place where anyone can see them.

Chapter 4 · 1 min

The evidence, part 3: what pricing power looks like over time

Buffett has said plainly what he looks for. In a 2010 staff interview with the US Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, he said: "the single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power." His test was practical. If you can raise prices without losing business to a competitor, you have a very good business. If you need "a prayer session before raising the price by a tenth of a cent," you have a terrible one.[5]

His clearest example is a candy company. In his 2007 letter, Buffett laid out the numbers for See's Candies, which Blue Chip Stamps, a company he and Charlie Munger controlled, bought in 1972.[4]

Volume grew at about 2% a year, Buffett wrote.[4] Sales grew more than twelve-fold. Sales per pound rose faster than consumer prices over the same 35 years, which means See's did more than keep up with inflation. Pre-tax profit went from less than $5 million to $82 million, and Buffett reported total pre-tax earnings of $1.35 billion over the period, with only $32 million reinvested in the business.[4]

Your business is probably not a boxed-chocolate brand, but the mechanism travels. When buyers believe in what they get, a fair price increase does not send them away.

Tip: Sales per pound is a crude price measure. It mixes price with product mix. Use the same kind of measure on your own business (revenue per unit, per tonne or per contract) and compare it with inflation over five years. If it has lagged, your pricing power has been leaking.

Chapter 5 · 1 min

The evidence, part 4: industrial prices since 2019

The years since 2019 were a live test of pricing power for manufacturers. Input costs jumped, and companies had to decide how much to pass on. The US producer price index tracks what domestic producers receive for their output.

Prices received by fabricated metal producers rose 56.7% between September 2019 and September 2026, and electrical equipment 50.6%, against 31.1% for consumer prices. Chemical prices rose sharply in 2021 and 2022, then gave some of it back in 2023, before rising again. Transportation equipment rose least, at 19.9%.

This chart does not show margins, and producer prices rose partly because costs rose. It does show that industrial buyers accepted large increases over six years, and that the increases held. Every one of those increases was a pricing conversation, and a supplier that could explain it in terms of value had an easier one.

A producer price index tracks prices for the same goods over time. It does not tell you any one company's price or margin.

Chapter 6 · 3 min

Original research: how 91 industrial companies handle price in public

The studies above describe price in general. We wanted to know how industrial companies, the sector we work in most, present price and value to buyers on their own websites. So on 3 November 2026 we scanned the public websites of 91 US mid-cap manufacturing and supply-chain companies, the same sample we used in our study of what AI says about you. The sample is every company in the S&P MidCap 400 and S&P SmallCap 600 lists classed under eleven manufacturing and freight sub-industries. The full method is at the end of this essay.

We looked for two things on each homepage: what a buyer could do next (request a quote, contact sales, find a dealer, configure a product, buy online, see a price), and what any dollar figure on the page was about.

Finding 1: the homepage talks to investors, not buyers

Of 91 homepages, 66 returned a readable page to our script. Three of those were script-only shells with almost no text, which left 63 homepages we could read.

Almost every homepage spoke to investors, job seekers and anyone wanting a general contact form. About one in four gave a buyer a specific next step. Only 7 of 63 (11%) offered a quote request on the homepage, and not one showed a product price.

This is not a failing in itself. Many of these companies are corporate parents whose product brands live on other sites, and most industrial products are quoted, not listed. But it does show where the homepage's attention goes. A buyer who arrives wanting to know what working with the company is worth finds plenty about the share price and little about the value of the product.

The sitemaps tell a similar story. Of 65 companies whose sitemaps we could read, 16 (25%) listed any page whose address pointed to a quote, a dealer locator, a store, a cart or pricing. Of the five store or cart pages we found, two showed prices in their raw HTML: a decking maker and a residential solar company, both selling to homeowners rather than to industrial buyers.

Finding 2: dollar figures describe the company, not the customer

Nineteen of the 63 homepages showed at least one dollar figure. We read each one in context and coded what it was about. A homepage could fall into more than one group.

Nine homepages used dollar figures for revenue, earnings per share, dividends, stock prices or bond offerings. Four announced contract wins or capital investments. Three reported charitable giving. One, a bearing and power transmission maker, used a dollar figure the way value-based pricing would: a worked example of five-year energy savings for a customer application, with the assumptions shown.

That single page is the exception that proves the point. Industrial companies know how to put a dollar figure on a homepage. They almost never use one to show what a customer gains.

Tip: Count the dollar figures on your own homepage and key product pages. How many are about you, and how many are about what your customer saves or earns?

What the scan does not show

We read raw HTML only, without running scripts, so content loaded later by JavaScript was missed. We checked homepages, not deeper product pages, where quote forms and calculators often live. Twenty-five homepages did not return a readable page to our declared research script, and we did not try to get around any block. The scan measures what a public visitor sees on one day, not how these companies set prices.

Chapter 7 · 1 min

A framework: the Price-Value Matrix

The evidence points to two separate jobs. One is making value visible to the buyer. The other is protecting the price once it is set. Most firms are stronger at one than the other, and the two together give four positions.

The way out of the commodity trap runs through one of the two middle boxes. In our experience, industrial firms more often sit in "hidden value." Their engineering is excellent and their price discipline is fine, but their proposals, websites and quarterly reviews describe the product, not the money it saves. The fastest move is usually to make the value visible.

Chapter 8 · 2 min

How to set a value-based price in five steps

The process below works for products and services, and you do not need special software to run it.

Step 1: Name the result in money

Write down what your best customers get, in their words and in numbers where you can: time saved, scrap avoided, downtime prevented, revenue gained, risk removed. Ask three or four of them directly what changed after they started working with you. Their words become your proof and your sales copy.

Step 2: Find the reference price

Work out what the buyer compares you with. It might be a competitor, doing the work in-house, hiring someone, or living with the problem. Then estimate what that alternative costs them. That number is your real competitor, and it is often far higher than another supplier's quote. People also weigh losses more heavily than equal gains, which is loss aversion, so "stop losing $2,000 a month to unplanned stoppages" lands harder than "save $2,000 a month."

Step 3: Set the floor and the ceiling

The floor is your full cost plus the minimum margin you need. The ceiling is the value from Step 1. Pick a price that keeps the buyer's gain obvious. Our rule of thumb is that the buyer should be able to see several times what they pay.

Step 4: Build three options

Offer a lean option, a main option and a premium option. The premium option makes the main one look reasonable, price-sensitive buyers get a way in, and buyers who want more can pay more. Put the option you most want to sell in the middle. A price that looks suspiciously low can also hurt, because buyers read price as a signal of quality, which the Hive explains under processing fluency.

Step 5: Test, then protect

Try the new price on new customers first and track three numbers: win rate, discount requests and profit per order. Then map your own price waterfall. List every discount, rebate, free service, payment term and freight allowance between list and pocket price, by customer. Figure 3 shows how much can disappear there.

Tip: Before you change a price, use Figure 2's arithmetic on your own margin. If a 5% price rise lifts profit per order by 40%, you could lose a fair share of buyers and still earn more.

Chapter 9 · 1 min

How to raise prices without losing good customers

Start with new customers, who never saw the old price. With current customers, give notice and a reason, and add something small, such as a quarterly review, so the new price reads as a new offer rather than a toll. Then show the value on a schedule. A short quarterly note on what you did and what it saved the client is the cheapest way we know to protect a price. It is also a good job for an AI flow: pull each client's numbers, draft the note, and have a person check it before it goes out, so the value stays visible across the whole customer base without adding hours for every new account. And accept a little churn. If no buyer ever says no, the price is too low.

Chapter 10 · 1 min

What this means for leaders

For manufacturing and supply-chain businesses, the data in this essay points in one direction. Price is the strongest profit lever (Figures 1 and 2), and the thinner the margin, the more each point of price is worth. Much of the price you set can leak away before it reaches you (Figure 3). And the public face of most industrial companies spends its dollar figures on investors rather than on what customers gain (Figures 6 and 7).

The same gap matters when a company grows. Expanding into a new region or a new buyer segment is the best moment to set a price on value, because nobody there knows the old price. It is also the moment value is hardest to show, because the new buyer has no history with you. The firms that expand well carry their proof with them: worked savings examples, customer results in local terms, and a pricing structure that does not have to be renegotiated market by market.

Another can adapt the same proof and the same three-option offer for each new market and language, with a person checking every version. Together they let a company take one well-priced offer to more buyers without a matching rise in headcount.

Chapter 11 · 1 min

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from cost alone. Cost is the floor, not the answer.
  • Discounting by default. A 1% discount at an average company removes 8% of operating profit.[2]
  • Ignoring the waterfall. Off-invoice terms, rebates and free services are price cuts nobody signs off on.
  • Hiding the value. If no page, proposal or review shows the buyer's result in money, the conversation falls back to price.
  • Offering one option. A single price gives a buyer two choices: yes or no.
  • Never revisiting the price. A price set five years ago was set for a different business.

The fix for most of these starts with positioning: who you serve and what you do better than anyone for them. A clear unique selling proposition makes value easier to see, and brand equity is the stored trust that lets you hold a price.

Methodology

Published data. Calculations on published figures (Figure 2's profit gain, Figure 3's invoice and pocket prices, Figure 4's sales per pound and CPI multiple, Figure 5's rebasing) are described under each figure and reproduced in the CSV files.

Sample. All 91 companies listed on Wikipedia's S&P MidCap 400 and S&P SmallCap 600 constituent pages under the GICS Industrials sector in eleven sub-industries: industrial machinery and components; building products; aerospace and defense; cargo ground transportation; construction machinery and heavy transportation equipment; electrical components and equipment; trading companies and distributors; agricultural and farm machinery; air freight and logistics; marine transportation; and industrial conglomerates. Official websites came from Wikidata (property P856), corrected by hand for five companies.

Scan. On 3 November 2026, a script requested each company's homepage, robots.txt and XML sitemaps (up to eight sitemap files per company). It identified itself with the user agent "BraveNewResearch/1.0" and a link to brave-new.com. It read raw HTML only, did not run JavaScript, and did not retry or try to get around any block.

Measures. A homepage counted as readable if it returned HTTP 200 with at least 50 words of text (63 of 91). Buying-path signals were matched by phrase in the page text and link text, then every match was read in context; five false matches were removed (for example, "request for quote" inside legal terms, and investor "historic prices"). Every dollar figure on the 19 homepages that showed one was read in context and coded by hand. Sitemap addresses were matched for quote, dealer, store, cart, pricing and configurator paths. Store and cart pages found this way were fetched once to check for visible prices.

Limits. This is one day and one sample of US-listed companies. Homepages of corporate parents may not reflect their product brands' sites. Content loaded by JavaScript and deeper product pages were not measured. Twenty-five homepages did not return a readable page to our declared script; we report them separately and do not count them in the shares.

The data behind every figure in this essay is published alongside it as CSV files.

Chapter 13 · 1 min

Frequently asked questions

What is value-based pricing?

Value-based pricing means setting a price from what a product or service is worth to the buyer, rather than from what it costs to provide. Cost still sets the floor. The buyer's result, such as money saved, downtime avoided or revenue gained, sets the ceiling, and the price sits between the two.

What is the difference between price and value?

Price is what the buyer pays. Value is what the buyer believes they get in return, in money, time, risk or status. Because value is a judgment, a seller can shape it with evidence: worked savings examples, customer results and clear comparisons with the buyer's alternatives.

How much does a 1% price increase raise profit?

At an average S&P 1500 company, McKinsey found a 1% price rise with steady volume lifted operating profit by 8%. The effect depends on margin. Using Damodaran's January 2026 data, it ranges from 6.3% in machinery to 24.4% in steel and 37.5% in basic chemicals, if volume and costs hold.

What is pricing power?

Pricing power is the ability to raise prices without losing business to a competitor. Warren Buffett called it the single most important decision in evaluating a business. It comes from buyers believing the product is worth more than it costs them, which is why visible proof of value protects price.

Should industrial companies publish prices on their websites?

Most do not. In our scan of 91 US mid-cap industrial companies, none of the 63 readable homepages showed a product price and 11% offered a quote request. Publishing prices suits standard products. For quoted products, publishing worked examples of customer savings does more to support price than a list price would.

How do you raise prices without losing customers?

Raise prices for new customers first, then for existing ones with notice and a reason. Add something small with the increase, and show the value regularly with a short report on what you did and what it saved. Expect to lose a few of the most price-sensitive buyers.
