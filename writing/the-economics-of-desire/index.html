Mimetic desire: why we want what others want, with data

By Anne Becheru, founder of Brave New Media.

Published 3 Nov 2026 · Updated 9 Nov 202618 min read

Start chapter 1 →Key findings ↓

Mimetic desire is the idea that people learn what to want by copying the desires of others, rather than by judging things purely on their own merits. The French thinker René Girard first set it out in Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque (1961), published in English as Deceit, Desire, and the Novel (1965). He called it "triangular" desire at first and later "mimetic" desire.[1] The idea explains a lot of buying behaviour that a simple price-and-features model cannot: the busy restaurant that gets busier, the brand everyone suddenly wants, the expensive option that sells better than the cheap one.

This essay brings together four bodies of evidence. The first is an experiment with 14,341 people that showed how visible popularity reshapes a market.[2] The second is a brain-scan study of how price changes the experience of the same product.[3] The third is a crawl of about 11,000 shopping websites that counted fake popularity and fake scarcity signals.[4] The fourth is our own study of how unequally public attention is shared among the brands in nine markets, and how attention moves when a famous "model" points at someone. We close with a framework, the Honest Desire Ladder, and a step-by-step method.

Idea in brief

The problem. Buyers rarely judge an offer alone. They look at who else wants it. When people in an experiment could see what others had downloaded, popular songs became more popular, and which songs won became harder to predict.[2]

Why it happens. Visible choices act as a model for the next buyer. Price acts as a signal too: people rated the same wine as more pleasant when told it cost more.[3] Because these signals work, some sellers fake them. One crawl found 1,818 dark pattern instances on 1,254 shopping sites, led by low-stock messages, countdown timers and activity messages.[4]

The gap (our data). Attention in markets is very unequal. Across nine Wikipedia categories of brands and manufacturers (1,554 entries), the top 10% drew a median 64.0% of 2025 pageviews. Industrial categories were as concentrated as consumer ones.

The answer. Make real demand visible, choose the right model for each buyer, use only real limits, and let price tell the truth about quality. The Honest Desire Ladder puts these in order.

Chapter 1 · 1 min

What mimetic desire is

Girard built the idea from novels. In his analysis of Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Dostoevsky and Proust, desire is rarely a straight line from a person to an object.[1] It runs through a third party, the model, whose desire the person copies. Don Quixote does not choose his ideals; he copies them from the heroes of the romances he reads.

Girard split models into two kinds. External models are far away in status, time or place, so you copy them without competing with them. Internal models are close: a neighbour, a colleague, a rival firm. You copy them and compete with them, and that is where envy and rivalry start.

Economists approached the same ground from the demand side. Thorstein Veblen's The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) gave us "conspicuous consumption", the idea that people spend visibly to show their standing.[5] A Veblen good is one that people want more as its price rises, because the high price is part of what they are buying.

Chapter 2 · 1 min

The evidence, part 1: visible choices reshape markets

The cleanest test of mimetic desire in a market is the MusicLab experiment by Matthew Salganik, Peter Sheridan Dodds and Duncan Watts, published in Science in 2006.[2] They recruited 14,341 participants, mostly from a teen-interest website, and asked them to listen to and download 48 songs by unknown bands.

Some participants made choices independently, seeing only band and song names. The rest were randomly assigned to one of eight separate "worlds" in which they could also see how many times each song had been downloaded by earlier participants in that world. Each world started from zero and evolved on its own. In a second experiment, the researchers made the social signal stronger by listing songs in order of popularity instead of in a random grid.

The results:

  • In both experiments, all eight social influence worlds were more unequal than the independent world: popular songs became more popular and unpopular songs less popular.[2]
  • Inequality rose further when the social signal was made stronger.[2]
  • Which songs won became less predictable. The same songs, heard by similar people, ended up with different rankings in different worlds.[2]

The authors summed it up this way: "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible."[2]

Limits of this evidence

  • Participants were mostly young people choosing free songs, not companies choosing a supplier.
  • The authors expected social influence in real markets, with marketing and media attention, to be "far stronger" than in their experiment.[2]
  • The paper reports inequality as a chart, so we cite its direction rather than its exact values.

Chapter 3 · 1 min

The evidence, part 2: price changes the experience

In a study published in PNAS in 2008, Hilke Plassmann, John O'Doherty, Baba Shiv and Antonio Rangel scanned the brains of 20 people while they tasted wine.[3] The participants were told they were sampling five different Cabernet Sauvignons, identified by price. There were only three wines.

People reported significantly higher pleasantness for wines 1 and 2 at the high price, and pleasantness was correlated with the stated price (r = 0.59). Activity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex, an area widely thought to encode experienced pleasantness, rose with price too.[3] In a follow-up session 8 weeks later, with no prices shown, there were no reported differences among the wines.[3]

For a seller, the lesson is narrower than it first sounds. Price did not change the wine. It changed expectation, and expectation changed the experience. The Hive's entry on the Coke and Pepsi brain study makes a related point about another famous scan, and Alchemy gathers many cases where perceived value beats a purely logical comparison.

Limits of this evidence

  • Twenty participants, one product, one lab.
  • The effect existed only while people believed the price. A product that disappoints teaches buyers to discount the signal.

Chapter 4 · 1 min

The evidence, part 3: when sellers fake the signals

Because popularity and scarcity signals work, some sellers fake them. In 2019, Arunesh Mathur and colleagues at Princeton crawled about 53,000 product pages from about 11,000 shopping websites.[4] They found 1,818 instances of dark patterns on 1,254 sites (about 11.1%), in 15 types. Because the crawler only explored product, cart and checkout pages and only text-based interfaces, the authors call this a lower-bound estimate.[4]

The three most common types all borrow the language of mimetic desire and scarcity: stock about to run out, an offer about to end, and other shoppers buying right now. Not every instance is a lie. A low-stock message can be true. So the researchers went further and tested which ones were deceptive.

Of 393 countdown timers, 157 (39.9%) either reset after they ran out or the "expiring" offer stayed valid. Twenty-nine activity notifications were generated at random or hard-coded. Seventeen low-stock messages counted down on a schedule or showed random stock levels.[4]

The US Federal Trade Commission's 2022 staff report, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, lists these practices by name: false low stock messages, false high demand messages and baseless countdown timers, with examples such as a message claiming only one item is left in stock, or an "Offer ends in 00:59:48" clock.[6] The report also cites a workshop study in which dark patterns doubled the share of consumers who signed up for a dubious identity theft protection service.[6]

Tip: If a customer would feel tricked on learning how a message was generated, do not show it. Regulators have named these practices, and the research shows they can be detected from the outside.

Chapter 5 · 3 min

Original research: how unequal is attention in a market?

The MusicLab study showed that visible popularity makes outcomes more unequal. We wanted to see what that inequality looks like in real markets today, including the industrial ones we work in most. Wikipedia pageviews give a free, public measure of how much attention each brand or company draws.

On 3 November 2026 we took every article listed directly in nine English Wikipedia categories: six consumer categories (luxury brands, watch brands, shoe brands, toy brands, cosmetics brands, car brands) and three industrial ones (machine tool builders, industrial machine manufacturers, pump manufacturers). For each of the 1,554 category entries (1,485 distinct articles, since a few appear in more than one category) we summed human pageviews for 2025, then measured how the attention was shared. The full method is at the end of this essay.

Finding 1: a few names take most of the attention

In the median category, the top 10% of articles drew 64.0% of all 2025 attention, the top 1% drew 22.5%, and the bottom half drew just 3.4%. Toy brands were the most concentrated: the top 10% took 86.4%, and one article, Labubu, drew 39.4% of the whole category's views on its own. That is the pattern the MusicLab worlds predicted: once a few names pull ahead, visible popularity keeps pulling attention their way. Car brands were the least concentrated, and even there the top 10% drew 47.4%.

Finding 2: industrial markets are just as unequal

The median Gini coefficient across the nine categories was 0.769. The three industrial categories had a median of 0.770, against 0.769 for the six consumer ones. Industrial machine manufacturers (0.847) were more concentrated than luxury brands (0.769), and in that category the top 10% of articles drew 81.9% of attention. We expected buyers of machinery and pumps to spread their attention more evenly than shoppers for watches and shoes. They did not. In machine tools, the most-viewed article drew 60 times the views of the median article.

Finding 3: attention follows the model

Girard's theory predicts that desire flows through models. One of the most visible public tests of that in recent years came on 24 September 2023, when Taylor Swift attended a Kansas City Chiefs game at the invitation of tight end Travis Kelce, as the Associated Press reported that day.[7] We compared daily Wikipedia views for Kelce and three related articles in the 28 days before and the 28 days from that game.

Kelce's median daily attention rose four times, and on 25 September 2023 his article drew 747,470 views, 29 times his median for the four weeks before. The effect spilled over to people and things next to him: his brother Jason, a Philadelphia Eagles center, saw his article rise 1.7 times, and the team's article rose 1.4 times. Views of Chiefs quarterback Patrick Mahomes rose only 1.3 times. The extra attention went to the person the model chose, not to the team around him.

What our data does and does not show

Pageviews measure looking, not buying. Wikipedia categories are built by volunteers, so membership is uneven, and some category members are only loosely related (a few large companies sit in categories we would not choose). Views of redirect titles are not included in the concentration study. The Kelce case is one event, and the weeks before it already included rumours, so the 4.0 multiple understates the jump from his normal level. Read these as pictures of how attention is shared and how it moves, not as market shares.

Chapter 6 · 1 min

A framework: the Honest Desire Ladder

The research points to an order of work. Each rung depends on the one below it. Social proof does nothing if the product disappoints, and scarcity is worthless if nobody wants the product in the first place.

RungQuestion it answersEvidenceWhat to check
5. Honest price signalDoes your price tell the truth about quality?Price raised rated pleasantness of the same wine; the effect vanished without prices.[3]Price against peers, what each tier includes, whether delivery matches the promise
4. Real limitsAre your scarcity messages true?157 of 393 countdown timers were deceptive in one crawl.[4]Capacity, batch sizes, seasons, seat limits stated plainly
3. The right modelIs your proof from someone your buyer wants to resemble?Attention followed the model, not the biggest star (our Kelce data).Testimonials and logos matched to each buyer type and market

Most teams start at rung 4, adding urgency because it is fast. The research suggests starting at rung 1 and climbing. Signals multiply real value; they do not replace it.

Chapter 7 · 2 min

How to use mimetic desire honestly: a step-by-step method

Step 1: Count your real proof

List every customer number, review, result and logo you could publish, with its source and your permission to use it. If you cannot back it up, leave it out. Keep this as a one-page proof file.

Step 2: Choose the model for each buyer

For each main buyer type, decide who they most want to resemble. In industrial and B2B sales, where our supply chain and manufacturing experience sits, the strongest model is usually a peer one step away in the same chain: a mid-sized distributor trusts another distributor more than a global brand. When you enter a new market, do this again, because buyers there may not know your current customers. Matching the right proof to each market and buyer type is one of the jobs we build AI flows for.

Step 3: Make real demand visible

Show customer counts that are accurate and current, named reviews with specific results, and "most chosen" labels on the option most people really pick. The MusicLab data suggests even a weak signal shifts choice.[2] The Hive's notes on Influence cover the strengths and limits of social proof.

Step 4: Write down your honest limits

List your real capacity, batches, seasons and seats. "We take four new projects a quarter" or "this batch is 2,000 units" is honest scarcity. A timer that resets is not. Scarcity borrows force from loss aversion, whose strength is debated, so lean on it lightly.

Step 5: Check your price signal

Ask whether your price tells buyers the right story. If you charge much less than peers of similar quality, buyers who cannot judge quality before purchase may read the low price as risk. Test a higher tier that really includes more.

Step 6: Run the "found out" test

Read every claim on your site and ask: would a customer feel misled if they knew how this was produced? If yes, rewrite it or remove it.

A made-up but typical case

This case is made up, but it's typical. Say a 60-person pump manufacturer sells to water utilities and wants to enter a new region. Its website shows logos of two global engineering firms and a "Limited offer" banner that never changes.

It runs the method. It replaces the global logos with three named utilities of similar size, each with a measured result. It removes the banner and states its real lead time and quarterly production slots. It adds a premium service tier with remote monitoring, priced above its standard tier. In the new region, it repeats step 2 with local reference customers. Nothing is fake, and every signal now points a buyer toward a model they recognise.

Chapter 8 · 1 min

What this means for leaders

For manufacturing and supply-chain companies, our data has an uncomfortable message: attention in industrial categories is as unequal as in consumer ones. A small number of names get most of the looking. Buyers who search a category see the leaders first, and their choices then confirm the leaders' position, as the MusicLab worlds did.

The research also shows a way in. In the music market, the winners differed from world to world, so early visible demand mattered a great deal.[2] And in the Kelce case, attention went to the person the model pointed at. For a challenger expanding into a new market, that means the first reference customers in that market are worth more than any campaign: they are the model the next buyers copy. The growth work is to win those first models, make their choices visible and repeat it market by market. That is the work we build AI flows for, so a small team can run proof, reference and outreach programmes in several markets at once.

Chapter 9 · 1 min

Common mistakes

  • Faking popularity. "Join 10,000 happy customers" when you have 800 is a lie, and fake activity messages can be found in a page's code.[4]
  • Permanent urgency. A countdown that resets teaches buyers to ignore you, and regulators have named it.[6]
  • The wrong model. Showing only global brands can tell smaller buyers you are not for them.
  • Raising prices with no reason. The price signal held only while the experience matched it.[3]
  • Copying luxury tactics wholesale. True Veblen goods rest on decades of status. Borrow the signal carefully.
  • Assuming quality wins on its own. In the MusicLab worlds, quality set limits, but visible demand decided much of the rest.[2]

Chapter 10 · 1 min

Methodology

Concentration study. On 3 November 2026 we listed every article (namespace 0) that is a direct member of nine English Wikipedia categories: Luxury brands, Watch brands, Shoe brands, Toy brands, Cosmetics brands, Car brands, Machine tool builders, Industrial machine manufacturers and Pump manufacturers. Subcategories were not included. For each article, a script requested monthly pageviews for January to December 2025 from the Wikimedia REST API (English Wikipedia, all access methods, agent type "user", which excludes known bots) and summed them. Articles with no recorded views counted as zero (35 of 1,554 entries). The script identified itself with the user agent "BraveNewResearch/1.0" and a link to brave-new.com; when the API asked it to slow down (HTTP 429), it waited and repeated the same request. For each category we computed the share of views going to the top 1% and top 10% of articles (rounded up to whole articles), the share going to the bottom half, and the Gini coefficient. Medians are across the nine categories.

Model effect study. Daily pageviews (same API settings) for Travis Kelce, Jason Kelce, Kansas City Chiefs and Patrick Mahomes from 27 August to 21 October 2023. We compared the median of the 28 days before 24 September 2023 with the median of the 28 days starting that day.

Limits. Pageviews measure interest, not purchases. Category membership is set by Wikipedia editors and is incomplete; a few members are large companies only loosely tied to the category. Views arriving through redirect titles are not counted. One event cannot show a general rule.

Chapter 11 · 1 min

Frequently asked questions

What is mimetic desire in simple terms?

Mimetic desire is the idea that people learn what to want by watching others. Instead of choosing purely on their own, they copy the desires of a model: someone they admire or compare themselves with. René Girard developed the idea in his 1961 book, published in English in 1965 as Deceit, Desire, and the Novel.

What is an example of mimetic desire in marketing?

Showing how many people chose a product is the simplest example. In the MusicLab experiment with 14,341 participants, letting people see download counts made popular songs more popular and made the final rankings less predictable. Testimonials, customer counts and "most popular" labels all work on the same principle.

What is a Veblen good?

A Veblen good is something people want more as its price rises, because the price itself signals status or quality. The name comes from Thorstein Veblen's 1899 book The Theory of the Leisure Class, which introduced conspicuous consumption. Research also shows price can change experience: people rated the same wine as more pleasant at a higher price.

Is scarcity marketing ethical?

Scarcity marketing is ethical when the limit is real, such as a fixed batch, set capacity or a seasonal product. It becomes deceptive when the scarcity is fake. One crawl of about 11,000 shopping sites found 157 countdown timers that reset or kept the offer after expiry, and the US FTC names false low-stock messages and baseless countdown timers as dark patterns.

How can a B2B company use mimetic desire?

Show proof from customers your next buyer wants to resemble, usually a peer of similar size in the same industry. Make real demand visible with named results and accurate numbers.
